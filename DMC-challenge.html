Overview

The Challenge

The big elephant in the room is that you have eight minutes to convey your thoughts. That means you have to be very critical about deciding which parts you show on the screen and which parts you say out loud. If you end up reading word for word from the slides, you are going to be in trouble.

You have to figure out what to say and what to show so that you convey more than eight minutes' worth of information. That's tough.

A general rule of presenting

“Don't show what you say, or say what you show.”

If you just put up a slide and read the words aloud, you'll have less than eight minutes' worth of content. What's on the screen and what you say should each add something the other doesn't.

Big suggestion

Don't talk about numbers and show numbers. That's too confusing. Put the numbers on the screen, and talk more loosely about what you think they mean or how you gathered them.

Target Audiences

The challenge calls for you to pick target audiences. But with eight minutes, you don't have time to break them down into four cross-tabbed, derived versions of who these people are.

  • Consumer behavior works very differently when you're only doing things online, because you can't reach most of those cross-tabs, especially when Meta's AI (or anybody's AI) is building the ads.
  • The suggestion: back off the cross-tabbed information and go loosely for gender, age, and geographic location.
  • Then build different personas by creating content that would apply to some of them individually, or to some of them in general.
  • The platform then looks for the right people. Or, based on who responds in the first day, it keeps looking for more people like them.
Backwards

That is a very backwards, consumer-behavior-looking approach compared with the traditional advertising books. Instead of picking the audience and then writing to them, the audience that responds tells you who the audience is.

Pick at least one of each

Remember, you have to have at least one consumer target audience (or persona) and at least one B2B one. The challenge doesn't specify where these come from.

Big Idea

The big idea has always been a foundation of advertising. It's how you sum up the entire campaign in just one phrase. Often that gets translated into taglines and slogans, like "Just do it."

But even Nike has realized that in an online world you can't keep repeating "Just do it" over and over and be successful. It gets lost in the noise, or people stop paying attention after the second or third time. Remember the Nike examples we looked at: they're pretty much all from the last year to year and a half. There are many different ways to say it.

You don't have much time on this challenge to include this, but it hints that you need something like it. Maybe it's an overall slogan.

The tough part

Your big idea has to work for the bakery, the food truck, online purchases by individuals, corporate giving, and the new automated retailing. That's tough.

An idea that sums up what we learned

The machines are spontaneous, while the other ways to buy (the bakery, trucks, cart, website orders, and gifting) are planned. In the survey, 52% ranked a special occasion as their most common reason to buy cupcakes, and only 10% ranked coming across them on the spot first (Q8). A big idea could hold both. A few to look at:

  • The joy of cupcakes: planned and spontaneous
  • Joy on the calendar. Joy on the corner.
  • Every plan. Every “why not?”
  • Order the celebration. Stumble into the joy.

This isn't a big part of the challenge. It's just something you should do.

Owned

Owned: Overview

Owned is everything the brand controls and doesn't have to buy media to use. The competition describes it as a proposed owned strategy that includes the website, the social accounts, and an email strategy. This page adds search and organic, and the machine itself, which can act as media.

The big point

For the most part, owned media is about conversion, not awareness.

The challenge asked for awareness and conversions. But people who reach your social media, your emails, or your website are already aware of you. Otherwise they wouldn't be searching for you or going to check you out.

So owned is mostly about getting people who barely know who you are, or who know you well, to take the next step: to find a different way to buy from you, for themselves, their family, or friends, or as part of a corporate gift.

The weight

Owned is worth 30% of the score. Taken literally, that's 2 minutes and 24 seconds.

What the audit found on social

  • Engagement is low, and you recognized that in your answers to the 105 questions.
  • With 4,479 Instagram followers, your median engagement is 0.46%. That is about half the 1.0% floor for accounts under 50K followers.
  • Instagram ranks posts by how likely each follower is to care, so weak engagement pushes your posts lower and fewer of your followers see them.
  • To reach the bottom of that range you would need about 45 likes and comments per post, roughly 2.2 times today. That's how you reach all of your people and take full advantage of social media.
  • We looked at your hooks and your content pillars. Founder and Behind the Brand posts average 81 likes and comments, three times the 26 for Promotional posts. Weekly flavor reveals and store-closure notices are what your followers aren't responding to.

What the audit found on the website

  • The look matches your feed, and Cupcakes in a Jar posts land on the right page in three taps.
  • But 2 of your 4 most recent offers, the specialty flavors and the $3 latte, appear nowhere on the site.
  • The founder and 10-year story is buried on the About page, and the homepage shows no store hours or this week's flavors.

A couple of suggestions

  • Lead with people. Make founder-led and community Reels a weekly slot, add collabs and customer reposts, and show people actually eating, sharing, and standing in line for the cupcakes.
  • Give every storefront post a landing spot. Add a "This Week at the Shop" page and make it the first bio link.
  • Put the truck, cart, and occasion boxes on the feed. None of your 30 most recent posts feature them.
Why do this?

If someone comes across your machine and wants to follow up, and they see an unengaged audience, they'll bounce and keep walking. You need to grab people and better tell your story: 11 years in business, a thriving business that sells through a bakery, food trucks, a cart, and online, plus the upcoming automated retailing. You have a lot to tell.

A big part of any redo

This is where influencer and creator content starts to work in. Show less of the actual product, and less of the descriptions for the content pillars already being used. Focus instead on people wanting to eat it, eating it, and sharing it.

Automatic replies to comments

A tool can read a comment and pick from several automatic replies. For example, someone comments "GIFT" and gets a reply plus a private message with a link to the corporate gifting page.

  • Owned, not paid. It runs on her own accounts, and you're not buying media. It costs a small tool subscription, roughly $10 to $20 a month, so give it a line in a small tools and miscellaneous budget.
  • It has to start with the person. It runs through Meta's official system and answers someone who commented or messaged first. It can't mass-message strangers, and free replies are limited to 24 hours after their message.
  • Why it helps: it sends people to the right page, and it can ask for an email address or phone number.

Where does automated retailing fit?

It's crowded right now, and it gets more crowded once you add something else. That's why you're trying to find a home for all of these things.

  • How many machine posts? Decide how many you'd really add on Facebook or Instagram. Facebook is an obvious place to adapt, because it currently repeats Instagram word for word (15 of 15 recent posts).
  • Maps: will you post maps of where the machines are? You may have room for that once every 3 or 4 weeks, and the feed gets even more crowded.
  • A better home: put a QR code on the machine that goes to a specific spot on the website showing a map of other locations, instead of crowding social.

  • Corporate gifting: a bulk-order path for the dozen, 25, or 100 buyers.

Right now: who is this for?

It's confusing who the site is for. Is it for people ordering for themselves or their family? Corporate Gifts is the left-hand tab and the first tile, so it looks like the major thing.

In the audit, the first 30 seconds showed a "Cupcake Bliss Sealed in a Jar" hero with Shop now, then tiles for Corporate Gifts, Cupcakes in a Jar, and Occasion Boxes, then the truck and the dessert cart. There were no store hours and no weekly flavors.

Five suggestions

The goal: someone arrives on the homepage and immediately knows where to go.

  1. Put a front door at the top. Ask one plain question, "Who are you shopping for?", and give four big, clearly named buttons: for me and my family, for my team or clients, for an event, and find a machine.

SEO + Organic

Being found when someone is already looking, without buying the click.

The problem with "cupcakes"

  • Very few people search for the word "cupcakes" or for "cupcakes near me."
  • When they do, the results are often recipes, grocery stores, and sellers with an inferior product.
  • So winning those broad searches is both unlikely and not worth much.

People search for their problem

  • People often search for the problem they've got: desserts, birthday treats, office party gifts, and things like that.
  • Make sure your Google Business Profile includes all of those things, so that regardless of what they're searching for, they find you.
  • Keying everything on the word "cupcakes" gets you only a fraction of the people who might be looking for you. Many of them aren't aware enough of you to include your business name in the search.

A few suggestions

  1. Go after the searches you can win. Be specific about what you sell and who it's for: cupcakes in a jar, corporate gift cupcakes, a cupcake truck or dessert cart for events, occasion boxes, and your town and region. Your corporate gifting blog post is the model. The audit found one blog post and no FAQ, and a search description that says "delivered in Merrillville," which undersells nationwide shipping.
  2. Send people to the right page, not the homepage. If someone finds you through a search, the link should land on the specific part of the website we already put up: the gifting page for gifting searches, the truck and cart page for event searches, the jar page for jars.
  3. Use the reviews you already have. The audit found 426 Facebook reviews to draw on. Show them on the pages people land on, and ask for new ones with the QR code on each machine.
  4. Make each machine findable. Where the platform allows it, give each machine location a local search listing, so someone near it who looks it up finds the address and the "find a machine" map.

Paid Google ads: same idea

The Google search ads they're paying for cover a wide variety of topics: corporate gifting, food trucks, cupcakes in a jar, plain cupcakes, and more. But they all map to the homepage.

Why not the homepage?

For paid ads, Google scores how well the landing page matches the ad and the search. That affects what you pay and where the ad shows. A homepage serves everyone, so it matches no one in particular. And since you're worried about conversions: a page that matches the ad can more than double the conversions of the people who visit.

Email

Email only works if you have addresses. So the first job is to gather emails, and there are many ways to do it. (And remember the question: how do they get the email?)

Ways to gather emails

  • After a website order. Once someone orders from the website, they've given you their email, and now you can follow up with reminders. Add a button at checkout asking whether they're buying for themselves or for a corporate gift or something similar. That is the difference between your red dots and your blue dots (consumer, B2B).
  • As part of the ad. The ad itself can ask for the email.
  • During a website visit. A sign-up on the pages people already land on.
  • At events. Take the food trucks out to farmers markets and other events. We would still list these under owned media, because you're in control of them and have very little expense.

What an event setup might look like

  • Spend $1,000 to $2,000 on a gazebo-style frame, a table, matching vinyl, and an iPad.
  • Ask people to fill in their information to get a discount on a cupcake, or to buy one, get one free at that farmers market.
  • They enter their email, or follow you on social. If they do, they get the reward.

Text messages

Texts sent to people who opted in through your own media are owned, just like email. You pay per message, but you aren't buying someone else's media.

  • Get permission first. A text list needs clear written opt-in, separate from the email box, and "Reply STOP" in every message. Business texting also has to be registered. Fines run $500 to $1,500 per violation.
  • The cost is small. About $0.01 to $0.05 per message, plus a small monthly fee and carrier charges.
  • People say they'd want them. 31% would want a bakery to reach them by text (Q15), and 22% prefer text for special-occasion orders (Q9a).

What you do with them

Once you have the emails, you get to reach out for a follow-up. That's not awareness, because they already know who you are. It's about increasing conversions among people who have already been in contact with you once or twice.

Two tracks

  • B2B: a sequence that moves a host or corporate buyer from a first inquiry to a signed machine or a standing gift order.
  • Consumers: alerts for new locations and offers.

The Machine

Once at least one automated retailing machine is in place, you get to use it to create something downstream. The machine is media you own, and it sits in a high-profile location all day.

The rendering

The challenge said to design, or have an AI render, what the machine might look like. Great, knock yourselves out, and avoid the generic AI-prompt look. But also think about what the machine does for the rest of the system. That starts with one QR code and a ticker.

One QR code

Include one QR code that people can snap. What would it take them to?

  • The website, or social media. That works, but a specific spot on the website would be better.
  • A page with a map of other locations.
  • A page that describes who the company is.

You can't load the machine up with five different QR codes. That's too confusing. So pick one. Where are they going to go, and what will you use it to integrate with the other pieces of the system?

A "who we are" page could say
Even though it's in a vending machine, there aren't a lot of preservatives in these. They're made by Midwestern hands, with Midwestern values and ingredients, and they're fresh. They last 30 days inside the machine without the need for heavy preservatives.
Careful with the 30 days

In the survey, 33% said a 30-day shelf life would make them less likely to buy, versus 28% more likely. Lead with fresh and made by hand, and let the 30 days follow.

The ticker

Another idea: a little box at the top of the machine with a red ticker, like the news or business tickers you see in many places, rotating through different messages. You might get more bang for your buck showing a rendering of the machine, with some of the phrases that could pop up beside it. Sky's the limit on what they say.

Paid: Overview

The big point

Paid is where you go get awareness.

Your owned media is mainly for conversions: keeping people in your loop, moving them to the next step, and giving them a way to get a reminder. Paid ads are mainly for reaching people who don't know you yet.

Organic social doesn't really provide awareness unless you are exploding with the highest engagement possible. The only people who find you are a small subset of your followers. Paid is where you reach out and add people to the system, or to your loop.

Start with the message, not the channel

Think about the message you want to get to people and what format it would come across best in.

  • Is it a video that someone will probably only watch the first 5 to 10 seconds of, like a Reel?
  • Is it writing? Something they can take in within 10 to 15 seconds, or something longer?
  • Do you need a longer video, a couple of minutes? Not for the mainstay of what you're doing, but maybe that's a reason to include something from YouTube.

Decide what you want to say, then figure out what technology you'll use. Don't start with "we have to use Instagram and we have to use Google Search."

Keep the paid breakdown short

  • You don't need to list 10 ways to reach people. You don't have time to talk about them. Four or five is fine.
  • For the 15-minute faculty presentation, add one or two more after the fact.
  • Don't use only social media and Google Search ads. She's already using those, and she wants to see something beyond them.
  • Faculty will notice if all your money goes there. Her social media isn't really working for her, and our web audit found almost no traffic going from social media straight to the website. So how would that work?

Think nationwide? Not yet

The challenge invites you to think beyond Chicagoland. But people who live close to her, in the Chicago area and the Midwest, are more likely to order from the website. That likelihood drops when you go nationwide.

  • So focus the next two years on building a monster base in this area first.
  • Then consider other markets, for automated retailing, corporate gifting, or buying for a party (graduation, birthday) or for yourself.
  • Those things would work in other places too. Chicago and Northwest Indiana is just the starting point.

In our survey, about 14% of people outside the Midwest said they were likely to order shipped jars from a bakery in another city or state, versus 21% to 22% in the Midwest (Q30). The outside-Midwest group is small (58 people), so treat it as a direction.

The weight

Paid is worth 30% of the score. Taken literally, that's 2 minutes and 24 seconds.

What the handout asks for

  • Justify your channels: why this one, for this audience, over the alternatives?
  • B2B: LinkedIn, paid search ("office amenities"), and account-based marketing aimed at facility managers.
  • Consumers: geofenced social near the sites, short video, and local creators.
  • Creative: three or more executions in three or more channels, with one consistent message that motivates action.
  • Think nationwide: phase in a scalable play once Chicagoland proves out (see above).
  • Budget and timeline: the $250,000 split by channel, and a 24-month Gantt chart. Past judges have debated whether this goes at the end of Paid or the start of Evaluation. Either works if it's clearly labeled.

Missed opportunities

LinkedIn

This one is for your blue-dot people, the B2B audience. It's frowned upon to sell consumer products on LinkedIn, and in some cases they don't even accept them. But if you're approaching people about corporate gifting, there are plenty of job titles you can target with an ad about how the product is great for corporate gifting, office parties, and company events.

Job titles you could target

  • Office managers and executive assistants, who often order for the team.
  • HR and people-operations managers, for employee appreciation.
  • Event and meeting planners, for office parties and company events.
  • Sales and account leaders, for client thank-you gifts.
  • Facility and property managers, if the goal is hosting a machine.

Pros

  • Aim by job title, company, and industry.
  • Reaches the people who order a dozen, 25, or 100 at a time.
  • Speaks directly to the blue-dot audience.

Cons

  • The most expensive per view and per click on the list.
  • Consumer products are frowned upon, and sometimes rejected.
  • A smaller audience, so people see the same ad again and again.
Typical cost: about $30 to $55 per 1,000 views (CPM) and about $5.50 to $12 per click (CPC), with a $10 a day minimum. See the pricing tab.

As with Google Search, send the click to the corporate gifts page, not the homepage.

Paid

Digital Ads (Display)

Banner and native ads that sit on other websites. A lot of people don't think digital ads work, or find them annoying. The common line is, "I'm more likely to get struck by lightning than to click on a digital ad." Some of that may be true, because engagement has traditionally been pretty low.

On the other hand, if someone is searching for birthday party ideas or corporate party ideas, they may be reading a blog or web page about exactly that. A well-placed ad can really stand out, because you already know what to put in the content.

Pros

  • Placed where the topic already matches what you sell.
  • The message can match the page exactly.
  • Cheap per thousand views.

Cons

  • Click rates are typically well under 1%.
  • Banner blindness, and some people find the ads annoying.
  • Your ad may land on weak pages unless you choose placements carefully.
Typical cost: about $1.50 to $4 per 1,000 views (CPM), and about $0.10 to $0.80 per click. See the pricing tab.

Where a well-placed ad could go

  • Birthday party ideas pages: you know the reader is planning a party, so the ad is about birthday treats or occasion boxes.
  • Corporate gifting pages: the ad is about gifting a team or clients.
  • "Unusual things to do in Chicago" or "fun and eccentric ideas to visit in Chicago": a great home for the novelty of the automated retailing machine.

Pinterest

Geofencing

Draw a virtual boundary around a venue. A provider uses permissioned mobile location signals to show ads in apps and on websites to people who are in or near it. Cute as a Cupcake doesn't need its own app. The ad might appear during the visit or later, and many visitors will never see one.

Best fit

Automated retailing, not website orders. Geofencing needs a physical place, and the machine is one. People ordering gifts and party boxes online are planning ahead, and search, Meta, Pinterest, and LinkedIn reach them better.

Pros

  • Reaches people near the machine, where an impulse buy happens.
  • Easy to test small, and some providers have no minimum budget.
  • No app of her own is needed.

Cons

  • Indoor location is rough. It can't reliably tell the floor or the machine.
  • A small lobby may have too few eligible phones.
  • Apartments and healthcare sites are restricted. Use signs and venue newsletters there.
Typical cost: about $5 to $15 per 1,000 views (CPM) as a planning range, not a quote. At $10, $1,000 buys about 100,000 impressions. Ask for an all-in price. See the pricing tab.

A pilot you could propose

  • Start at one busy, eligible site, like a mall or an accessible office building.
  • Name the exact spot in the ad: "Cupcakes available now near the east entrance."
  • Pair it with signs and venue communications.
  • Compare machine sales with a similar site that gets no ads. A visit to the building doesn't prove a cupcake purchase.

Influencers

For heaven's sake, of course she's going to need other people talking about her as well. So you probably want to introduce influencers.

Not in week one

As we've mentioned in class, you probably don't want to start with this in week one. The first several months should be about housekeeping: cleaning up, getting organized, redoing the website, and re-coming up with how the content pillars fit together.

  • Does she use all those different formats?
  • Does she try to combine two topics into one post so they can appear more frequently?

Then, maybe in month four, maybe in month six, maybe more toward the end of the first year, whatever you think is appropriate, you start to get other people talking about her. That's where influencers come in.

Pros

  • Other people talking about her, not just her own accounts.
  • Local people who can reach local audiences, like Chicago.
  • Content you can reuse on your own pages.

Cons

  • Too early, it distracts from fixing your own house first.
  • Price and quality vary a lot from person to person.
  • Reach depends entirely on that person's following.
Typical cost: about $50 to $500 for a specific post from a local creator. $1,000 may get 3 or 4 posts from one influencer. See the pricing tab.

What it might cost

You can always Google how much it costs. Honestly, someone who talks about things to do in Chicago is probably paid about $50, $250, or $500 for a specific post.

So you could easily designate $1,000 starting in month four or month six for influencers, and give a very brief synopsis of what that would look like.

Find one person, and show them

  • No one expects you to go through 100 influencers, and you don't have time to talk about more than one or two.
  • Find one person who seems appropriate for Cute as a Cupcake.
  • Take a screenshot of that person that shows their reach, their cost, and the topics they talk about.
  • Put that on the screen while you talk about influencers in the paid section.

Evaluation

Media + Evaluation: Overview

What the challenge says

Media is all placement, schedule, and budget. Evaluation is the KPIs.

This section is worth 15%. It's more than the typical evaluation work of how you'd measure success for awareness and conversions. It also carries your budget numbers, which is why it comes right after paid.

The weight

Taken literally, that's 1 minute and 12 seconds. This gets crowded quickly, so choose what you show and what you say.

What you need

  1. Budget: a pie or bar chart of the $250,000 across four to six things, with the dollars, the percent, and what each buys.
  2. Reach: impressions are not awareness. Report what happens at three and seven views.
  3. Gantt chart: 24 months, January 2027 through December 2028, that show movement.
  4. Awareness + conversions: how much of each you expect to generate.
  5. KPIs: how you'd measure them.

Past judges have debated whether the budget and timeline go at the end of Paid or the start of Evaluation. Either works if it's clearly labeled.

Evaluation

Reach: Impressions Are Not Awareness

If you spend $1,000 a month on Meta at $10 per thousand, that buys 100,000 impressions a month. But one impression doesn't make someone aware. Some people will blindly claim that seeing something once means you're aware, and that's not the case at all.

So don't report only the big number. Report impressions, then divide them to show what happens at three views and at seven.

Example numbers from the Budget tab. Impressions run into the millions, and the number of people who saw it often enough to remember is far smaller.

Do the math with the Pricing sheet

The Pricing sheet on Canvas calculates these for you. For example, $1,000 a month on Meta at a $10 CPM gives about 100,000 impressions a month (2,400,000 over 24 months). And $1,000 a month on Meta clicks at $1 per click gives about 1,000 clicks a month (24,000 over 24 months).

Takeaway
  • Report at least three views, and seven if you can.
  • This is simple division. It assumes every person sees the ad the same number of times, and that the channels don't overlap.
Evaluation

Gantt Chart

Show movement

If every line says "we'll do this every month," everybody will stop reading.

That's like handing over 12 social media ideas with nothing about week two or week three. A Gantt chart has to show progression over the 24 months, or nobody will take it seriously. What happens in month 12? In month 20?

  • Some lines are steady. $2,000 a month on Meta awareness and $1,000 a month on Meta clicks, almost constant, bumped up a little at key peaks.
  • Some go dark. Most farmers markets close in late fall through winter, so those months are blank.
  • Some build. The machine barely exists in the first months, then grows. Rebuilding owned media happens first, so creators don't start until later, maybe month 6, or toward the end of the first year.

When you present it

For heaven's sake, don't read every line.

It's a trap a lot of students fall into, and you'll burn more than a minute doing it. Point out highlights instead.

  • The steady lines. "As you can see, we're running Meta ads for awareness, Meta clicks, and Google Search pretty much every month for the whole 24 months. They're the bread and butter."
  • The darker bars. "These are months we've identified for running even more."
  • One more thing that stands out, like something that pops up seasonally. Then move on.

Don't say, "15% of our budget goes to Instagram, it's this much per month, it's this many impressions, divided by 3 and divided by 7." You could take eight minutes just to explain this chart.

If viewers like your ideas, they'll slide back through the video and pause at 6:30, 6:52, or 7:00 and read it themselves. So you don't need to transcribe everything on the Gantt chart, the budget chart, or anything like them.

Evaluation

Awareness + Conversions

This is where you estimate how much awareness and how many conversions you will generate. It takes a few minutes to explain, so decide what goes on the screen and what you say.

Awareness

For the most part, we already know the starting awareness level. The survey asked: before today, had you ever heard of Cute as a Cupcake?

Where people liveHad heard of herPeople surveyed
Merrillville area57%63
Rest of Northwest Indiana38%64
Chicago and its suburbs8%218
Everywhere else1%113
Everyone17%458

Northwest Indiana combined is 47% (127 people). Chicago city alone is 8% (134), and its suburbs are 7% (84).

Traditionally, you would survey awareness before and after. We can't repeat the survey for another year or two. So the most common approach is to report, from the budget, how many impressions or exposures you will deliver. Focus on how many people will see you 3 times or 7 times from the CPM efforts, because those are the awareness-building ones.

The comparison

Once a projection says "your budget of this equals this many impressions, with people seeing you 3 or 7 times," that is clearly far more awareness than you have right now. In the example on the Reach tab, that's about 4.2 million people at three views and 1.8 million at seven.

Conversions

Most websites convert at less than 2%, probably about 1.75%. With our suggestions you could easily double that and might approach 4%, which would count as good or maybe even great.

2026 benchmarks differ by source, but most land between about 1.4% and 3%. The top 20% of Shopify stores convert at 3.2% or more, and the top 10% at 4.7% or more.

Count every clickable moment

  • Someone clicks a QR code and goes to the website.
  • Someone clicks an ad you paid for by the click (Meta CPC) and goes to the website.
  • Someone clicks a Google Search ad and goes to the website.

Add those up, then apply the conversion rate, so you can say you're also going to generate this many conversions.

Takeaway
  • Awareness: impressions turned into people who saw you 3 or 7 times, compared with the starting awareness.
  • Conversions: every click, scan, and ad visit counted, times a conversion rate.

Benchmarks

  • Compare before and after, and against industry: CPM, CTR, cost per lead, and conversion.
  • Be honest. Name the risks and say how you would overcome them. The first machine has to work and be profitable before the plan moves on.
