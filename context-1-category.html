Project Context · 01

The Category Changes the Marketing

A bag of chips, a mattress, and a Tesla are bought in three completely different ways. Before you can judge a brand's marketing, you have to know what its category demands of it.

01
The Argument

The Category Sets the Rules. The Brand Decides How to Play.

Most weak case studies start with the brand: its personality, its value proposition, the trend it should lean into. That reasoning is backwards. The category was being bought a certain way long before the brand showed up. A bag of chips, a mattress, and a luxury handbag are not bought, advertised, or won the same way, and the brand that ignores this loses to one that does not.

Before you judge a brand's IMC choices, know what its category demands of it. Five questions get you most of the way there. (How an individual buyer moves through a decision is Unit 4B's territory; this page sits one level up, at the category.)

02
The Diagnostic

Five Questions That Reset Everything Downstream

1. How often is the category bought?

Toothpaste runs out every few weeks. Mattresses are replaced roughly every eight years (Better Sleep Council, 2023). Frequency decides whether you are maintaining mental availability across thousands of small purchase moments (Sharp, 2010), or intercepting a buyer during one deliberate search window that opens and closes within a few weeks.

Tip High-frequency categories lean on steady, ongoing brand presence. Low-frequency categories need a persistent brand presence plus concentrated performance media inside the window. Frequency changes the shape of the media plan, not just its size.

2. What occasion or trigger creates demand?

Some categories are routine (detergent runs low). Some are event-driven (a new baby, a move, a wedding). Some are seasonal (gyms in January, fragrance in December). The trigger tells you when the window opens, and therefore when paid media stops being wasted on buyers who are not yet in market.

Tip Event-driven categories often pulse media around the trigger. Seasonal categories build awareness weeks ahead and shift to conversion inside the season. In an occasion-driven category, the calendar matters as much as the budget.

3. How much risk, research, or explanation precedes purchase?

Nelson distinguished search goods, judged before purchase (a shirt's fit), from experience goods, judged only by using them (a hotel stay) (Nelson, 1970). Darby and Karni added credence goods, which buyers cannot fully judge even afterward (a financial advisor, a vitamin) (Darby & Karni, 1973). A $3 yogurt needs almost no consideration. A $3,000 mattress takes weeks.

Tip High-research categories need real middle-funnel investment: reviews, comparison content, demos, retargeting. Low-risk impulse categories can run largely on shelf presence and distinctive assets. If buyers do weeks of homework, the brand has to stay in their memory the whole way through.

4. Are the buyer and user the same person?

Baby food is bought by a parent and eaten by a baby. B2B software is bought by procurement and used by employees. The communication target is whoever holds the wallet, but the experience target is the user, and most failures come from speaking only to the one who pays (Andrews & Shimp, 2018). Check whether your brand is quietly talking to only one of the two.

5. Is success driven by habit, comparison, trial, or social meaning?

Detergent is habitual: you buy what you bought last time. Car insurance is comparison-driven: you shop. Craft beer is trial-driven: you sample, then settle. Sneakers and watches are won partly through social meaning, what owning the thing says about you. Most categories mix several. Identify the dominant lever, because the lever decides where the money goes.

Tip Habit categories need disruption to break in (sampling, distinctive packaging, deals). Comparison categories reward visibility at the moment of search. Trial categories reward samples and first-purchase incentives. Social-meaning categories reward owned media, community, and cultural association. The lever changes the PESO mix.
03
In Practice

Two Brands, Two Categories, Two Different Games

Pringles
High Frequency, Low Risk, Habitual
  • Bought weekly or every few weeks
  • Zero research, near-zero consideration
  • Won on shelf presence, distinctive assets, and the next purchase moment
  • Mental availability is the whole game
Tempur-Pedic
Low Frequency, High Risk, Comparison
  • Bought roughly once a decade
  • Reviews, in-store trials, financing decisions
  • Won during a four-to-six-week shopping window
  • Brand-building keeps the name worth remembering when the window opens

Both are mature, well-known, nationally advertised brands. Their budgets, funnel coverage, and use of search look almost nothing alike. Treating them as if they had the same job is a mistake that gets confidently delivered in a lot of pitch decks.

In Your Project

Category dynamics is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics. If it is one of the two or three that shape your brand's situation, argue it there, and let it constrain your Part 2 judgments.

  • Funnel coverage. Habitual, frequent categories live mostly at the top of the funnel. Comparison-driven, infrequent categories need heavy middle-funnel work. Judge your brand's funnel in Part 2 against what its category demands.
  • The 60/40 read. Don't assume a high-consideration category justifies more performance spend. Binet and Field found the optimal brand share tends to rise in high-consideration categories, peaking in financial services (Binet & Field, 2018). What changes is when performance money is spent: concentrated inside the search window.
  • PESO emphasis. Trial-driven categories lean on earned media, habit-driven categories on paid, social-meaning categories on owned and shared. These are responses to category conditions, not a definition of good marketing.
  • Place the foil on the same board. Your focal brand and foil share a category, so differences in how they play it are choices, and those choices are worth a sentence each.

Research Prompts

Short on time? Start with the Google searches, then use the source list to go deeper. Use the Think It Through prompts only after you have the data.

Quick Google Searches

  • "[category] purchase frequency" · "how often do people buy [category]"
  • "[category] consumer journey" · "[category] buying process research"
  • "[category] seasonality" · "when do people buy [category]"

Find the Facts

  • Category data: NielsenIQ, Circana, Mintel, and Statista (through the library)
  • Trade publications and industry associations (e.g., Beverage Digest, BedTimes, the Better Sleep Council) often publish purchase-cycle data
  • Annual reports and investor decks of the category's large players sometimes disclose buyer and frequency benchmarks

Think It Through

  • "Given that [category] is bought every [frequency] with [low/medium/high] research effort, what does that imply about funnel coverage for a brand competing in it?"
  • "Is [category] won mainly through habit, comparison, trial, or social meaning? What evidence supports that call, and how do [brand] and [foil] each play it?"

Vocabulary

Glossary

Search good
A product whose qualities can be evaluated before purchase: color, size, price, specifications. Easy to compare, low informational risk.
Experience good
A product whose qualities are revealed only after use: a restaurant meal, a hotel stay, a streaming service. Reviews and trial periods matter disproportionately.
Credence good
A product whose qualities the buyer cannot fully verify even after consumption: legal advice, vitamins, structural repairs, a financial planner's competence. Trust signals carry the weight.
Habitual purchase
Repeat buying with little deliberation. The brand chosen last time is usually the brand chosen this time. Mental availability and distribution carry the win.
Mental availability
The probability that a brand comes to mind in a purchase moment. The Ehrenberg-Bass formulation popularized by Byron Sharp. Distinct from awareness, which only requires the buyer to recognize the brand when prompted.
Listening window
The period during which a buyer is actively in-market and receptive to category messages. Long in mattresses and cars. Short in chips and gas.
