Project Context · 02

Launch, Scale, Defend, or Revive?

Most growth problems are misdiagnosed because the brand and its category get analyzed as if they were the same thing. Three questions, asked in order, separate them, and the answer usually picks the communications objective for you.

01
The Argument

Stop Asking Whether the Brand Is Mature

The textbook product lifecycle (introduction, growth, maturity, decline) treats the stage as a property of the brand alone (Levitt, 1965). It collapses two different conditions into one fuzzy label. A new brand can enter a mature category. An old brand can sit inside a fast-growing one. A leader can be quietly declining while a challenger in the same category doubles. Treating those as the same situation produces the same generic plan. Ask three questions instead, in order.

02
The Diagnostic

Three Questions, In Order

1. What is happening to the category?

Emerging: small, fast-growing, rules still forming. Growing: demand expanding, competitors arriving. Mature: demand saturated, brands fighting for share. Declining or disrupted: shrinking, or losing buyers to an adjacent category (landlines, traditional cable).

Tip In growing categories even average brands grow, because the tide is rising. In mature categories, growth has to be taken from other brands. Diagnose the category first, because it sets a ceiling on what the brand's plan can achieve. Appendix A is where this gets sized.

2. What is happening to the brand?

Launching: building first awareness and trial. Scaling: a proven product expanding buyers, geography, or distribution. Defending: an established position under pressure. Reviving: a faded brand coming back. Two less common states round out the list: entering a new segment or use case, and harvesting a decline for cash. Part 3's growth-stage condition uses the first four.

Tip A brand and its category can be in completely different states: Apple Vision Pro launching in an emerging category, Liquid Death scaling in a mature one. The IMC plan depends on the intersection of the two.

3. What growth lever is available?

There are seven classical levers: more buyers (penetration), more occasions or use cases, higher frequency among existing buyers, bigger basket, higher price or richer mix, new geography, or a new product. McDonald's adding all-day breakfast is an occasion lever; Liquid Death moving from convenience into grocery and club is a channel and geography lever. Most brands have one or two levers genuinely available. The others sound plausible in a pitch and fail in the field.

Tip Penetration favors broad reach and brand-building. Frequency favors retention email, loyalty, and reminder media. Occasion expansion favors use-case content. Geography means starting awareness from zero. Identify the dominant lever, because the budget should weight toward it.
03
In Practice

Four Brands, Four Different Problems

Launch · Apple Vision Pro
Big Brand, Emerging Category
  • Spatial computing is not yet a defined category
  • Marketing's first job is category education, not differentiation
  • Lever: creating category buyers, not taking share
Scale · Liquid Death
Challenger, Mature Category
  • $333M revenue in 2024, in 133,000-plus US doors (Sacra, 2026)
  • Bottled water is mature and dominated by Coke and Pepsi
  • Lever: penetration. Brand-heavy, because the product needs no explaining
Defend · Coca-Cola
Leader, Mature Category
  • Decades of distinctive assets carry most of the load
  • Lever: share defense plus modest occasion expansion
  • Consistency and freshness inside the same codes (see Page 7)
Revive · Stanley (Quencher)
Faded Brand, Mature Category
  • A century-old work brand turned fashion item by one tumbler
  • Lever: convert a cultural surge into a durable position
  • The risk: peaking visibly, then falling visibly
04
Theory into Execution

Penetration, and Why Targeting Width Matters

The Sharp and Ehrenberg-Bass research introduced in Unit 2A is the scaffolding here. Across hundreds of categories, sustained brand growth comes more from adding buyers than from extracting more from existing ones (Sharp, 2010). Retention still matters, especially for subscription and B2B brands (Sharp & Romaniuk, 2016). The two are sequential, not competing: growth requires acquiring buyers, and retention decides how valuable they become.

The execution consequence is targeting width. Wide targeting reaches all category buyers regardless of intent: expensive per conversion, but the only way to build memory among people not yet in market. Narrow targeting reaches audiences with known intent (past visitors, cart abandoners, subscribers): efficient, but it only captures demand that already exists. That is roughly what the 60/40 split names. 60/40 is less a budget rule than a width-and-temperature decision dressed up as a percentage.

The student instinct is "more targeted is better." At the brand level it is often the opposite. A brand that over-targets looks efficient while quietly shrinking its future buyers. Conversion goes up. Growth stops.

Tip In a segment expansion, the width question becomes explicit: narrow the buy to the new segment, or widen to all category buyers and let positioning sort it out? Narrowing looks efficient. Widening gives the brand room to grow. Cash-constrained brands usually narrow; well-funded brands usually widen.
05
The Bridge

From Growth Problem to Measures of Success

Once you know the growth problem, the communications objective often picks itself. A challenger scaling in a mature category needs awareness and trial. A leader defending share needs consistent presence and distinctive assets. A reviving brand needs to turn a moment into a position. Three levels of objective are routinely confused:

Level What it names Example
Business objective The financial or share outcome the company cares about Grow revenue from buyers aged 35 to 50 by 12% in 12 months
Communications objective The change in awareness, perception, or behavior the plan can produce Increase consideration among buyers aged 35 to 50
Media metric A measure of activity, not outcome Reach 60% of the segment at adequate frequency

Typical communications objectives: build awareness in a new segment, drive trial, shift consideration or perception, increase frequency, add occasions, defend share, reposition on an attribute, or shift from acquisition to retention. Pick one as primary. A campaign that tries to do all eight does none of them.

Part 3 asks for two or three measures of success, each with a metric, a time horizon, and the place the number would actually come from. Tie at least one to the business objective and one to the communications objective. A media metric alone does not show the plan worked.

In Your Project

Growth stage is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics, and it is the lens for one of the four Part 3 conditions.

  • Part 1. Name the category's state and the brand's state separately, for both the focal brand and the foil. Point to Appendix A for the sizing.
  • Growth-stage shift (Part 3). If you pick this condition, the brand moves from scaling to defending or defending to reviving. Say which lever changes, which objective replaces the old one, and what that does to the 60/40 and channel mix. Appendix D shows the before and after.
  • The 60/40 direction. Brand-heavy is the default when the lever is penetration. Performance-heavy is defensible when the job is converting an already-aware audience.
  • Measures of success. Use the table above. Reach is an input; a consideration shift or share change is an outcome.

Vocabulary

Glossary

Penetration
The share of category buyers who buy your brand in a given period. The central growth metric in Sharp's framework. Bigger brands almost always win on penetration.
Growth lever
The mechanism by which a brand can grow: more buyers, more occasions, more frequency, bigger basket, higher price, new geography, or new product. Most brands have one or two genuinely available at any moment.
Business objective
The financial or share outcome the company is trying to produce: revenue, profit, share. The thing the CFO cares about.
Communications objective
A stated change in awareness, perception, or behavior that an IMC plan is designed to produce: build awareness, drive trial, shift consideration, increase frequency, defend share.
Media metric
A measure of media output (reach, impressions, clicks, video views), not an outcome. Confusing media metrics with objectives is one of the most common planning mistakes.
Targeting width
The breadth of the audience a media buy is aimed at. Wide targeting reaches all category buyers regardless of intent; expensive per conversion but the only way to grow mental availability. Narrow targeting reaches a defined audience with known intent or behavior; efficient per conversion but limited in scale.
Audience temperature (cold, warm, hot)
A media-buying convention that groups audiences by prior interaction with the brand: cold (none), warm (some engagement), hot (past purchasers, cart abandoners, subscribers). Cold audiences roughly carry brand-building; hot audiences carry conversion.
