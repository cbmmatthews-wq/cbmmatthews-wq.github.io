Where Does the Brand Sit, and What Does Its Price Signal?

A label like "premium" or "niche" carries more weight in a marketing plan than it should. Three separate questions sit underneath it, and they sometimes disagree.

01
The Argument

One Label, Doing Too Much Work

Case studies usually sort a brand into mass, premium, niche, or luxury. Those labels conflate three separate dimensions that often disagree. A product can be premium but sold everywhere, niche but hard to find, or expensive without being meaningfully better. Each combination implies a different IMC plan. Ask three independent questions, then read the combination.

02
The Diagnostic

Three Axes, Not One

1. Market scope

How big a tent is the brand pitching? From mass market (designed for everyone) through broad differentiated (a clear segment focus) and specialist or niche (a narrow buyer with specific needs) to prestige (a deliberately small slice that pays disproportionately). This is the segmentation question in different clothes, and stretching along it (a mass brand launching a premium line, a niche brand chasing mass) is a common source of growth failure.

2. Availability

How easy is the brand to find and buy? Ubiquitous (supermarkets, drugstores), selective (a limited retailer list), exclusive (boutique-only, by appointment), or intentionally scarce (the Birkin, limited-release sneakers). Availability is not scope: Tide is premium in its category and sits in every supermarket. It is also one of the few axes a brand controls directly, through distribution (Page 4).

3. Price tier

Where does the price sit relative to category alternatives? This is the most consequential axis for IMC, because it shapes discounting, channel mix, and how hard the brand can lean on performance media.

03
Price Tiers

The Five Tiers (and Why They Are Comparative)

A tier label means something only inside a category. A $75 notebook is prestige in stationery. A $75 mattress is discount in bedding.

  • Discount. Clearly below the category norm; wins on price. Mass retailers, value channels, warehouse clubs.
  • Value. At or slightly below the norm with a clear benefit story. Not "cheapest," but "defensible deal." Kirkland is the textbook case.
  • Mainstream. Inside the broad acceptable range, where most established brands live. Differentiation and distinctive assets do the work, not price.
  • Premium. Clearly above the norm with a defended, tangible difference in performance, materials, or experience.
  • Prestige or luxury. Well above the norm, with a difference that includes symbolism, scarcity, and status, not only performance.
Tip Not sure which tier? Watch how the brand discounts. Discount brands discount constantly, value brands strategically, mainstream brands in cycles, premium brands carefully, and prestige brands almost never. If list price and selling price have drifted apart, the brand's real tier may be lower than its positioning claims (Page 6). The longer treatment of price bands lives on the Zone of Indifference page.
04
The Key Distinction

Premium versus Prestige

Premium brands promise tangible better: Hoka over a generic running shoe, the Westin over a Holiday Inn. The argument is "pay more, get more, and here is what more means." Prestige brands promise that plus symbolism, scarcity, and status. A Birkin is not just a better bag; it is a signal that travels when carried (Kapferer & Bastien, 2012).

The common mistake is assuming prestige brands should restrict everything, including communications reach. They often want enormous reach, because broad fame is part of what makes the brand prestigious. Millions of non-buyers need to recognize the brand for the few buyers to feel the value of owning it. What prestige restricts is product availability, presentation, and discounting. A luxury brand can want mass awareness without mass ownership. Bain and Altagamma estimate the global luxury customer base shrank by roughly 50 million buyers between 2022 and 2024, even as luxury's cultural visibility stayed high (Bain & Altagamma, 2025).

Tip Premium or prestige? Ask whether the brand's value depends on what owning it says about the owner. If yes, you are closer to prestige, and more exposed to damage from discounting and uncontrolled availability.
05
In Practice

What Each Tier Changes for IMC

Tier Where the IMC plan usually weights
Discount Performance media, price-led messages, retailer co-op support, frequent sales promotion
Value Benefit and comparison content, light strategic promotion, balanced brand and performance
Mainstream Distinctive assets, broad reach, periodic promotion, roughly the default 60/40
Premium Brand-building, proof points, controlled presentation, careful discounting
Prestige Owned and earned media, cultural placement, broad fame with controlled availability, discounting rare or avoided

A discount brand spending like a prestige brand runs out of money before it runs out of competitors. A prestige brand running discount tactics erodes the position its price was meant to defend.

In Your Project

Position and price tier is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics, and it is also how you choose and check your foil.

  • Check the foil. The handout asks for a rival in the same category and price tier. Place both brands on all three axes. If the foil sits a tier away, pick a different foil before the milestone.
  • Constrain Part 2. Tier sets the tone, creative register, and promotional behavior you should expect. In the brand identity section, judge whether the brand's visual and verbal identity matches its tier.
  • Constrain Part 3. A segment expansion that pulls the brand toward a different tier needs to say what changes in price, distribution, and discounting. A budget cut or competitor shock should not quietly solve itself with discounts a premium brand can't afford to run.

Research Prompts

Short on time? Start with the Google searches, then use the source list to go deeper. Use the Think It Through prompts only after you have the data.

Quick Google Searches

  • "[brand] price vs [foil]" · "[brand] vs [foil] which is better"
  • "[category] price tiers" · "best [category] brands premium vs budget"
  • "[brand] sale" · "[brand] promo code"

Find the Facts

  • Price benchmarks: walk the brand's and foil's own sites plus two or three retailers that carry the category
  • Discount history: the brand's email list, Honey or RetailMeNot, and Meta Ad Library for price-led ads
  • Luxury and prestige: Bain & Altagamma's annual luxury market study (free); Kapferer & Bastien's The Luxury Strategy

Think It Through

  • "Place [brand] and [foil] on market scope, availability, and price tier. Where do they match, where do they differ, and are the three answers for [brand] consistent with each other?"
  • "Is [brand]'s discounting consistent with its stated tier, or is it slowly eroding the price signal?"

Vocabulary

Glossary

Market scope
How broadly the brand targets buyers. Mass, broad differentiated, specialist or niche, prestige.
Availability
How easy the brand is to find and buy. Ubiquitous, selective, exclusive, intentionally scarce. Controlled directly through distribution.
Price tier
Where the brand's price sits relative to category alternatives. Discount, value, mainstream, premium, prestige. Comparative, not absolute.
Premium
Priced above the category norm with a defended tangible difference: performance, materials, service, or experience. The brand argues for better.
Prestige (or Luxury)
Priced significantly above the norm with a difference that includes symbolism, scarcity, status, and cultural meaning, not only performance. Highly sensitive to uncontrolled availability and discounting.
Zone of Indifference
The price band inside which a brand can move without losing buyers or signaling something new. Bounded above by perceived overpricing, bounded below by perceived quality risk. Covered in depth on the linked Zone of Indifference page.
