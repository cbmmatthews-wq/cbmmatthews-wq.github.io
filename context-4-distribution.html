Project Context · 04

DTC, Retail, or Both?

Direct-to-consumer was sold for a decade as the future of retail. The data does not show that the future arrived. It shows that the model has costs the original pitch hid, and that e-commerce and DTC are not the same thing.

01
The Argument

Two Confident Claims That Need Correcting

Two claims show up in nearly every conversation about modern retail: "e-commerce is taking over" and "DTC is the future." Both hold a grain of truth inside a larger error.

E-commerce was 16.9% of US retail spending in the first quarter of 2026, growing faster than retail overall (9.8% versus 3.9%) (Census Bureau, 2026). But roughly 83 cents of every retail dollar is still spent offline. And about 40% of US e-commerce runs through Amazon alone (eMarketer, 2025).

The bigger confusion: e-commerce is not DTC. Most online sales happen on Amazon, Walmart.com, Target.com, and other retailer sites. A shoe brand selling through Zappos is doing wholesale e-commerce, not brand-owned DTC. True DTC is a much narrower slice. When students write that "DTC is taking over," they almost always mean e-commerce.

02
The Taxonomy

Five Distribution Models

  • Brand-owned DTC. The brand's own site and stores. Maximum margin, data, and control; maximum operating cost.
  • Wholesale to retailers. Target, Sephora, REI. The retailer buys at wholesale, marks up, and owns the customer at the point of sale.
  • Marketplace. Amazon, Walmart Marketplace, Etsy. The platform runs discovery and payment; the brand runs the listing and the ad spend.
  • Distributor. Common in beverages, food, and B2B. Handles logistics and retailer relationships for a substantial cut.
  • Franchising or licensing. Operator partners pay royalties (restaurants, fitness, some apparel).

Most established brands run several at once. Hoka sells through specialty running stores, REI, Dick's, its own site, and international distributors. The mix is a strategic bet, not a neutral description: it accepts retailer power in wholesale, acquisition cost in DTC, or platform dependence in marketplace.

03
Where the Margins Sit

The Value Chain on One Product

Illustrative but consistent with industry norms: the brand sells to a wholesaler at about double its cost, the wholesaler marks up 20 to 30%, and the retailer applies keystone pricing (doubling the wholesale price).

The retailer carries the markdown risk. A 20% discount on the $24 sticker cuts its margin from $11 to about $6; a 50% clearance takes it to roughly break-even. That risk is what the brand pays someone else to carry.

04
The Economics

What DTC Actually Costs

The DTC pitch was that removing the retailer lets the brand keep the retailer's margin. Removing the retailer does not remove the work the retailer was doing. The brand inherits it.

Wholesale
What the Retailer Does
  • Generates traffic and discovery
  • Runs payment, fraud, and customer service
  • Warehouses, ships, and handles returns
  • Merchandises the shelf or the page
DTC
What the Brand Inherits
  • All demand generation, paid and earned
  • Site operations, payments, and service
  • Inventory risk, fulfillment, and returns
  • Discovery without a shelf

The funnel has no free top

A wholesale brand at Target gets discovery almost free: foot traffic the retailer paid for, shelf placement next to alternatives, "frequently bought together" suggestions online. None of that exists for a DTC brand. It pays for awareness, pays for consideration, and pays to convert the click. This is why DTC and e-commerce-led brands often need to spend a larger share of revenue on marketing than wholesale brands (see Page 5).

DTC and the 60/40 Split

The handout's quick guide says to lean toward performance when selling DTC. That's true in one sense: DTC is where conversion happens and gets measured, so a larger share of spend naturally lands in performance media. The catch is that performance media only harvests demand that already exists. Binet and Field call the belief that online buyers are best reached with online activation messages the "online fallacy" (Binet & Field, 2018). A DTC brand with no retailer to generate demand for it has to fund its own brand-building too, and a common story in the trade press is the DTC brand that went nearly all-performance and watched acquisition costs climb once the easy buyers were gone.

CAC and LTV: the test DTC has to pass

CAC (customer acquisition cost) is what the brand pays to win one buyer. LTV (lifetime value) is the margin that buyer produces over time. A common rule of thumb is an LTV-to-CAC ratio of at least 3 to 1. DTC raises CAC directly, so the model works only if repeat purchase, subscription, or price justifies it. Many DTC brands failed because they assumed LTV would cover CAC and discovered, slowly, that it would not.

Tip In a DTC brand's financials, look for the lines wholesale brands mostly don't carry: fulfillment, freight, returns, and paid marketing as a share of revenue. If marketing runs 25 to 35% of revenue and gross margin isn't exceptional, the model is straining.
05
A Cautionary Case

The Allbirds Reality Check

Allbirds was the DTC darling of the late 2010s and went public in 2021 at roughly a $4 billion valuation. Net revenue fell 23.3% year over year in the third quarter of 2025; the company closed its remaining US full-price stores in early 2026 and in April 2026 agreed to be acquired by American Exchange Group for $39 million (Allbirds, 2025). The lesson is not that DTC failed. Its specific mistakes (store over-expansion, an unclear product line, promotions that eroded the premium price) were its own. The general lesson is that DTC was sold as a structural advantage and turned out to be a channel choice with its own costs. The model is not a moat.

In Practice

What Each Model Changes for IMC

Model Where the IMC plan usually weights
Brand-owned DTC Performance media, retargeting, retention email, owned media, customer data, plus the brand-building no retailer will do for it
Wholesale Shopper marketing, retail media (Walmart Connect, Amazon Ads, Target Roundel), trade promotions, packaging-as-advertising
Marketplace Marketplace search ads, listing optimization, review management
Distributor Trade marketing, distributor incentives, in-store support, less direct consumer spending
Hybrid / omnichannel Channel coordination, conflict management, integrated planning across paid, owned, trade, and retail media

In Your Project

Distribution is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics, and it shapes nearly every Part 2 judgment about the marketing machine.

  • Part 1. Name the channel mix for both brands. If the foil sells through a channel your brand doesn't, that difference probably explains part of the gap in their marketing.
  • Part 2 and Appendix C. For a DTC-heavy brand, the click path in Appendix C is the store. Judge whether the social promise survives the landing page. For a wholesale brand, look for shopper marketing and retail media, not just social.
  • The 60/40 read. Heavy DTC pushes spend toward performance, but ask who is funding demand creation. If nobody is, say so.
  • Pricing across channels. Heavy promotion in one channel without coordination in others erodes the price signal everywhere, and creates channel conflict with retail partners.

Vocabulary

Glossary

DTC (Direct-to-Consumer)
Selling directly to the end buyer through brand-owned channels (own website, own stores). Removes the wholesaler or retailer from the transaction, but not the work that party was doing.
E-commerce
Any retail transaction completed online, regardless of seller. Includes Amazon, Walmart.com, Target.com, retailer sites, marketplaces, and brand DTC. Roughly 16.9% of US retail in early 2026.
Keystone pricing
A retail markup convention in which the consumer price is set at double the wholesale price (a 100% markup). Common in apparel, accessories, and softer-goods categories; less common in grocery, where margins are far thinner.
Omnichannel
A coordinated strategy where multiple channels (DTC, wholesale, marketplace, retail) work together rather than competing. Requires deliberate planning and conflict management.
Channel conflict
The tension that arises when a brand's channels compete with each other on price, exclusives, or audience. Most acute when wholesale partners feel undercut by the brand's own DTC pricing.
Shopper marketing
Marketing activity aimed at the point of purchase: in-store displays, packaging, in-aisle media, retailer-specific promotions, co-op advertising. Predominantly a wholesale-brand discipline.
Retail media
Paid advertising sold by retailers on their own properties (Walmart Connect, Amazon Ads, Target Roundel, Kroger Precision Marketing). One of the fastest-growing categories of digital ad spend.
CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)
The total marketing and sales cost to win one new buyer. In DTC, a first-order line on the income statement. In wholesale, much of the equivalent work sits inside the retailer's margin.
LTV (Customer Lifetime Value)
The total margin a customer is expected to produce over the time they keep buying from the brand. A common health benchmark is an LTV-to-CAC ratio of at least 3 to 1.
