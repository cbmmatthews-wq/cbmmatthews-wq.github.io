The 5 to 10 Percent Rule, and Why It Needs an Asterisk

Most sources say brands reinvest roughly 5 to 10% of revenue in marketing, more often 8 to 10%. That is a useful starting point and wrong in most specific cases, because the right number depends on the brand's goal, its margin, and who it is competing with.

01
The Rule

Where the 5 to 10 Percent Comes From

Two surveys anchor the rule. Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey, mostly of companies over $1 billion in revenue, put the average marketing budget at 7.7% of revenue, with half of CMOs at 6% or less (Gartner, 2025). The CMO Survey from Duke, Deloitte, and the AMA, which includes more smaller firms, put it at 9.4%, and at 15.5% for B2C product companies (The CMO Survey, 2025). Small-business guidance attributed to the SBA has long suggested 7 to 8% of gross revenue for firms under $5 million with healthy margins.

The rule is a large-company, steady-state rule. The percentage moves with the brand's goal. A brand trying to grow, launch, defend against a rival, or enter a new segment usually spends above its steady-state share, or pulls money from somewhere else to fund the push (see Page 7).

02
Definitions Matter

What "Marketing Spend" Actually Means

Before comparing any number to a benchmark, know what is being counted. "Marketing spend" can mean the total budget (media, agencies, martech, staff, creative, research), paid media alone, trade and promotional allowances, or sponsorships. In Gartner's data, paid media is only about 31% of the total, or 2.4% of revenue. A "7% marketing budget" and a "2% media budget" can describe the same company.

First-purchase discounts are another trap. Many brands record them as a reduction of revenue rather than as marketing expense, so a brand's reported marketing percentage can leave out a large trial-discount program entirely (see the box on Page 6).

Tip "[Brand] spends 8% on marketing" means nothing until you say what's included. If you can't find a clean disclosure, say so and label your estimate on the Evidence Ladder.

04
The Underlying Mechanic

Margin Sets the Ceiling

The goal sets how much a brand wants to spend. Gross margin sets how much it can. Gross margin is what's left after the cost of making the product; marketing, overhead, and profit all come out of it. Contribution margin goes further, subtracting per-sale costs like shipping and returns, and is often much lower in DTC.

LVMH runs roughly 66% gross margin and spent about €9.5 billion on advertising and promotion in 2024, around 11.5% of revenue (Robin Report, 2025). A grocery retailer at 25 to 30% gross margin and 2 to 4% net margin cannot spend 11.5% of revenue on marketing and survive. That is the link from price tier (Page 3) to budget: a premium brand's margin is what unlocks its marketing, and moving down-market collapses both.

Tip Before recommending more marketing, estimate the brand's gross margin. Most "the brand should spend more" recommendations collapse once the margin comes out.
05
In Practice

Three Real Deviations from the Rule

Startup or DTC turnaround: 20 to 40%

Allbirds reported marketing expense of $32.3 million on $105 million of revenue in the first nine months of 2025, or 30.8% (Allbirds, 2025), roughly four times the large-company average. That is the signature of a DTC brand paying for its whole funnel (Page 4) while trying to spend its way back to growth.

Luxury and lifestyle: 10 to 30%

LVMH's 11.5% is sustainable because margin permits it, and the spend is part of what justifies the price. Cut the marketing and the premium starts to erode. Lifestyle brands sit higher still: Red Bull is widely reported (never disclosed) to spend 25 to 30% of revenue, mostly at the top of the funnel, because buyers are partly buying the brand.

Low-margin retail and mass: 2 to 4%

Walmart, Costco, and Kroger spend enormous absolute sums that are small as a share of hundreds of billions in revenue, and they lean on foot traffic, scale, and private label. A startup that benchmarks against Walmart's ratio is reading the wrong benchmark. Ratios compare fairly only across similar margin structures.

In Your Project

Spend level is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics, and it is the baseline for every Part 3 condition.

  • Part 1. Estimate where the focal brand and the foil sit against the right benchmark (large incumbent, startup, luxury, low-margin) and say why. State what you're counting.
  • Part 2. Your 60/40 read sits on top of this number. A brand spending 25% of revenue with a 60/40 split is a very different machine from one spending 4%.
  • Part 3. Every condition changes the budget: a 30% cut, a push into a new segment, a competitive response. Defend any move above or below benchmark with a reason, and pressure-test it against gross margin.

Find the Facts

  • SEC EDGAR 10-Ks: many companies report advertising or marketing expense and gross margin directly
  • Earnings call transcripts (Motley Fool, Seeking Alpha) often discuss marketing as a percentage of revenue
  • Benchmarks: Gartner CMO Spend Survey (free summary) and The CMO Survey (cmosurvey.org)
  • For private brands: estimate from observable media activity and a margin assumption, and label it Level 4

Think It Through

  • "What is [brand]'s reported or estimated marketing spend as a share of revenue, what does it include, and how does it compare with [foil] and the right benchmark?"
  • "Given [brand]'s gross margin and goal, what range of marketing spend is sustainable, and what would a 30% cut or a competitive push do to it?"

Vocabulary

Glossary

Marketing budget
The total annual cost of running a brand's marketing function. Includes paid media, agencies, martech, internal labor, creative production, and research. Distinct from advertising or paid-media budget alone, which is a subset.
Advertising (or paid-media) budget
The portion of marketing spend that goes to placing paid ads in media. About 31% of the total marketing budget on the Gartner 2025 average, or 2.4% of company revenue.
Gross margin
Revenue minus the cost of goods sold, as a percentage of revenue. The first ceiling on what marketing can sustainably cost. A 70% gross-margin brand has far more room to spend on marketing than a 25% gross-margin brand.
Contribution margin
Gross margin minus variable costs per unit (shipping, transaction fees, returns). What each sale actually contributes toward fixed costs and profit. Often meaningfully lower than gross margin, especially in DTC.
