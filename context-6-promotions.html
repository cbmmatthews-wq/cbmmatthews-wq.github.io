Project Context · 06

The Sale Tag Has a Payer

When a $26 sweater goes on sale at 30% off, someone is paying for that $7.80 discount. Producer, retailer, wholesaler, or a negotiated mix. Knowing which one matters more than students usually realize, because it changes who has the leverage and what the discount is actually doing to the brand.

01
The Argument

The Discount Is Real Money. The Payer Decides What It Means.

Sales promotion is one of the largest lines on a consumer-goods P&L and one of the least understood. Two distinctions clear most of it up: consumer versus trade promotions, and who actually pays. Pull those apart and the assumption that heavy discounting is a sign of strength usually falls apart too.

02
The Taxonomy

Two Types of Promotions

Aimed at the Shopper
Consumer Promotions
  • Coupons and first-purchase codes
  • Temporary price reductions and BOGO
  • Samples and trial sizes
  • Loyalty offers and rebates
Aimed at the Retailer
Trade Promotions
  • Off-invoice allowances (a temporary wholesale price cut)
  • Scan-backs (paid per unit sold during the promotion)
  • Display fees, co-op ads, and slotting fees
  • Volume incentives and markdown support

Consumer promotions move shoppers. Trade promotions move retailers, who may pass the value on to shoppers or keep it as margin. A brand funding a trade promotion has limited control over whether the discount ever reaches the shelf.

03
The Mechanics

Who Actually Funds the Discount?

It depends on the mechanism, and it is often shared. A retailer clearing end-of-season stock usually eats the markdown itself. A manufacturer's off-invoice allowance is manufacturer money, whether or not the retailer passes it through. Loyalty-card promotions and co-op ads are typically split by contract (often 50/50 or 75/25). A distributor may run a discount but charge it back to the brand. Following the dollars is the only way to know whether a promotion is sustainable or quietly destructive.

Trial Discounts: Marketing Budget or Cost of Doing Business?

Many brands, especially DTC and subscription brands, invest heavily in first-time trial discounts to get buyers into the fold: 20% off the first order, a free first box, a free sample. Some brands count that money inside their 8 to 30% marketing budget. For many others it is an additional sum on top, treated as a cost of doing business.

The accounting is part of the reason. Discounts and coupons are usually recorded as a reduction of revenue, not as marketing expense. Beyond Meat's 10-K, for example, records coupons, temporary price reductions, and other promotions as a reduction of revenues (Beyond Meat, 2026). So a brand's reported marketing percentage can leave out a large trial-discount program entirely. When you estimate your brand's spend, ask whether the discounts are inside the number or on top of it.

04
The Reality Check

Trade Promotions Are Huge, and Most of Them Lose Money

McKinsey found that CPG companies invest around 20% of revenue in trade promotions annually, often the second-largest line after the cost of goods (McKinsey, 2019).

The reasons are structural. Events repeat because they are familiar, retailers demand support brands can't easily refuse, and few companies measure how many promoted units would have sold anyway. If your brand's category runs heavy on trade spend, don't assume the spend is producing growth.

05
A Cautionary Case

How a Discount Habit Quietly Damages a Brand

Case · The J.Crew Discount Spiral

In the early 2010s J.Crew was a culturally dominant specialty retailer. After a 2011 leveraged buyout added roughly $1.7 billion in debt, it promoted more and more aggressively, until customers had been trained to expect 70% off and, as one retrospective put it, frequent discounting taught them to wait for a markdown (Marie Claire, 2020). The list price had become fiction. The company filed for Chapter 11 in May 2020. The pandemic was the trigger; the discount spiral was the condition.

The mechanism is reference-price erosion: each promotion teaches buyers what the product is "really" worth, and the next full price looks inflated. Premium positioning unwinds first, then quality perceptions, then margin.

06
In Practice

What Promotions Actually Do (and Don't)

  • Trial. The legitimate strategic use: get a first-time buyer to try the product. Works when the product earns repeat purchase on its own.
  • Subsidized purchases. Discounts to buyers who would have bought anyway create no new sales. The hardest thing in promotion to measure.
  • Stockpiling. Buyers load up, and next month's sales drop. Demand is pulled forward, not added.
  • Cannibalization. The promoted item steals from the brand's own other products rather than from competitors.
  • Training the buyer to wait. Predictable cycles teach shoppers when the next markdown comes.
  • Conflict with premium positioning. Every visible discount erodes a prestige or premium position (Page 3).

In Your Project

Promotional posture is one of the six Part 1 diagnostics: how much volume moves on discount, and who pays for it.

  • Part 1. Audit frequency, depth, and predictability of discounts for both brands. Note whether first-purchase offers and trade support sit inside or outside the brand's marketing spend.
  • Part 2. Check whether promotion is consistent across channels and with the price tier. Heavy discounting in one channel and none in another creates channel conflict.
  • Part 3. If your foil cuts price, Page 7 covers whether to match. Under a budget cut, don't quietly replace brand-building with discounts a premium brand can't afford to run.

Vocabulary

Glossary

Consumer promotion
A promotional offer aimed at the end shopper: coupons, rebates, BOGO, loyalty offers, samples, sweepstakes. The shopper sees and responds to the offer directly.
Trade promotion
A promotional offer or allowance aimed at the retailer or distributor: off-invoice allowances, scan-backs, display and feature allowances, co-op advertising, slotting fees, volume incentives. The shopper may never see these directly, but they often fund the in-store discounts the shopper sees.
Trial discount
A first-purchase offer (a code, a free first box, a sample) meant to bring new buyers in. Usually booked as a reduction of revenue, so it may not appear in a brand's reported marketing expense.
Reference price
The price a buyer expects to pay for a category or brand, formed through experience. Repeated discounting moves the reference price downward, after which the list price stops functioning as the brand's actual price signal.
Incrementality
The portion of promoted sales that would not have happened without the promotion. The opposite of subsidized demand. Notoriously difficult to measure cleanly, which is part of why so many trade promotions lose money.
Stockpiling (pantry loading)
Buyer behavior in which a shopper purchases more units than usual during a promotion, then buys nothing for an extended period. Future demand pulled forward rather than added.
Cannibalization
Sales of a promoted product that come from the same brand's other products rather than from competitors. Total brand revenue unchanged or down; individual SKU revenue up.
