Project Context · 07

Leaders, Challengers, and the Response

A marketing budget is never big or small on its own. It is big or small relative to what the competition is spending, what the brand's share already is, and what the brand is trying to do next. Know your position before you decide whether to spend more, spend less, or spend differently.

01
The Argument

Your Budget Is Measured Against Someone Else's

Page 5 asked what a brand can afford to spend, given its margin. This page asks a different question: how much does the brand need to spend, given who it is competing with and what it wants to happen? A brand that wants to grow, a brand defending share from a fast-moving rival, and a brand moving into another brand's segment face three different answers, even at the same revenue.

Two ideas carry most of the weight. The first is that share of voice predicts share of market. The second is that well-known brands still fade when they go quiet. Together they explain why leaders keep advertising, why challengers usually overspend relative to their size, and why a competitor's big push rarely can be ignored for free.

02
The Diagnostic

Share of Voice versus Share of Market

Share of market (SOM) is the brand's slice of category sales. Share of voice (SOV) is its slice of category advertising. John Philip Jones showed that the two track each other, and that brands whose voice exceeds their market share tend to grow (Jones, 1990). The gap is called excess share of voice (ESOV). Binet and Field's analysis of the IPA effectiveness databank put a rough number on it: each 10 points of ESOV is associated with about half a point of market-share growth per year (Binet & Field, 2013).

The rule cuts both ways. A brand spending below its share of market is, on average, slowly giving share back, even if this year's sales look fine. That is the quiet logic behind the familiar ranges: a leader holding 35% of a category can often hold position by spending close to its share, while a new brand with 2% share has to talk far above its size to be noticed at all. It is also why startups routinely spend 20% or more of revenue at launch. They are buying voice ahead of the share they hope to earn.

Tip You will not find your brand's SOV disclosed. Estimate it from what you can observe (Meta Ad Library, Google Ads Transparency Center, TikTok Creative Center, visible sponsorships), and use relative search interest in Google Trends as a rough proxy for share of attention. Label the result as a Level 4 inference on the Evidence Ladder. A defended estimate of "roughly at share" or "well below share" is enough to drive the argument.
03
The Two Postures

Leaders Defend Memory. Challengers Buy Attention.

Leader
Defend the Position
  • Spend at or slightly above share to hold ground
  • Keep distinctive assets consistent; freshen execution, not codes
  • Reach the whole category, including new buyers every year
  • Block credible attacks early, often by launching the counter-product yourself (Ries & Trout, 1986)
  • The mistake: copying the challenger's voice and abandoning your own
Challenger
Break Through the Noise
  • Spend above share, or win disproportionate earned and shared attention
  • Concentrate: one segment, occasion, region, or channel at a time
  • Attack where the leader is weak, not where it is strong
  • Integrate owned, earned, and shared media so each paid dollar travels further
  • The mistake: spreading a small budget across the leader's whole battlefield

Why famous brands still need reminding

Students often assume a brand everyone knows can stop advertising. The evidence says otherwise. Ehrenberg-Bass researchers tracked brands that stopped broad-reach advertising for a year or longer: sales fell an average of 16% after one year and 25% after two (Hartnett et al., 2021). A follow-up study of 365 US brands found market share declining at a steady rate each unadvertised year (Phua et al., 2023). Big brands decline more slowly, but they decline. Memories fade, retailers favor advertised brands with shelf space, and every year brings category buyers who never saw last decade's campaigns.

04
The Response

A Rival Is Taking Share. Match, Ignore, or Flank?

When a competitor makes a big move (a celebrity campaign, a deep price cut, a new line, a retailer deal), there are three basic responses. Each has a cost, and the right one depends on what the rival is attacking.

Response When it fits What it costs
Match The rival is attacking your core buyers, and you can afford to hold share of voice Budget, margin (if matching price), and the risk of legitimizing the rival
Ignore The move is temporary, costs the rival more than you, or targets buyers you don't need Share, if you misjudged it. Silence is cheapest only when you are right
Flank The rival owns the high ground, so you compete on a different segment, occasion, or benefit New product, new message, or both, and the chance of splitting your own brand

Whichever response you choose, the money has to come from somewhere. A brand can raise total spend (defensible if margin permits, see Page 5), or it can hold the total and reallocate: pull from a lower-value segment, a weaker channel, or a region, and concentrate where the threat is. The same logic applies to segment expansion. Moving into a new segment usually means moving into someone else's territory, and the incumbent there has the voice advantage. Either the budget rises or something else gives.

Tip A price cut is the move most often matched by reflex and most often regretted. Matching a rival's discount can hand both brands a lower reference price (Page 6). Ask first whether the rival can sustain the cut longer than you can afford to ignore it.
05
When the Budget Shrinks

Protect Reach and Memory. Cut What Would Have Sold Anyway.

A budget cut is a competitive decision disguised as a financial one, because the cut lowers share of voice while rivals keep talking. The going-dark research gives a clear order of priorities. Protect reach among category buyers and the distinctive assets that make the brand recognizable. Reduce frequency beyond what is needed, and retargeting aimed at people who were already going to buy. Kill fragmented one-off activations and channels that duplicate each other. Performance spend often looks like the safest line to keep because its results are easiest to count, yet some of what it counts would have happened without it.

This is also where integration pays for itself. A brand whose owned, earned, and shared media genuinely work together can often cut paid spend further before share of voice collapses, because the unpaid channels are carrying part of the load.

06
In Practice

Energy Drinks: One Leader, One Incumbent, One Challenger

The US energy drink category shows all three postures at once. Red Bull, widely reported (though never disclosed) to spend 25 to 30% of revenue on marketing (see the Intro), sits alongside Monster at the top. Celsius Holdings spent three years climbing into a clear third place.

Case · US Energy Drink Dollar Share, Q1 2023 to Q1 2026

Monster (portfolio): 38.9% to 32.6%. Red Bull: 36.9% to 34.7%. Celsius Holdings (portfolio): 8.1% to 20.9%. Figures are Circana tracked-channel dollar share, as reported in a Celsius investor presentation (Celsius Holdings, 2026a).

Much of the challenger's jump was bought, not only earned: Celsius acquired Alani Nu in April 2025 and Rockstar in August 2025, with PepsiCo leading its distribution (Celsius Holdings, 2026b). Alani Nu's pull with young women drew a flanking response from Monster, which chose to launch its own female-focused, zero-sugar line, FLRT, in March 2026 rather than buy Alani Nu (Food Business News, 2026).

Read the case through this page. Monster lost the most share and answered with a new line aimed at the challenger's strongest segment: a flank. Red Bull lost less and, based on its observable behavior, kept its own playbook, which is consistent with a leader defending memory rather than chasing a rival (a Level 4 inference). Notice also the source: the share figures come from the challenger's investor deck. Celsius's own number is a disclosure. Its rivals' numbers are third-party data presented by a competitor, and should be labeled that way.

In Your Project

This page matters most for two Part 3 conditions, but the leader-or-challenger call belongs in Part 1 for every team.

  • Name the posture in Part 1. Is your focal brand the leader, an incumbent, or a challenger relative to your foil? Is it spending roughly at, above, or below its share? That call constrains everything in Part 3.
  • Competitor shock. Your Appendix D matrix should show all three responses and their costs, then defend one. Say where the money comes from: new budget or reallocation, and from what.
  • Budget cut, 30%. Your protect / reduce / kill list should protect reach and distinctive assets first, and defend every kill with a reason, not a preference.
  • Segment expansion. If the new segment belongs to someone else, estimate the voice you will need there and say what you pull to fund it.

Research Prompts

Short on time? Start with the Google searches, then use the source list to go deeper. Use the Think It Through prompts only after you have the data.

Quick Google Searches

  • "[category] market share by brand 2026" · "[brand] market share"
  • "[brand] vs [foil] market share" · "[foil] new product launch 2026"
  • "[brand] advertising spend" · "[brand] Meta Ad Library"

Find the Facts

  • Market share: 10-K filings, investor presentations, and earnings calls often cite Circana or NielsenIQ share, including rivals' share
  • Voice: Meta Ad Library, Google Ads Transparency Center, and TikTok Creative Center for both brands over the same period
  • Attention proxy: Google Trends comparison of the focal brand and the foil over three to five years
  • Competitive moves: trade press (AdAge, Marketing Brew, Modern Retail, category trade titles) for launches, price moves, and retailer deals

Think It Through

  • "Given [brand]'s estimated share of market and the voice we observed, is it spending above, at, or below share? What does that predict for the next two years?"
  • "If [foil] made [specific move], what would matching, ignoring, and flanking each cost [brand], and which buyers would each response protect?"

Vocabulary

Glossary

Share of market (SOM)
The brand's percentage of total category sales, usually measured in dollars or units over a defined period.
Share of voice (SOV)
The brand's percentage of total category advertising. Classically measured in media spend; today often estimated from observable ad activity, impressions, or search interest.
Excess share of voice (ESOV)
Share of voice minus share of market. Positive ESOV tends to precede share growth; negative ESOV tends to precede share loss.
Going dark
Stopping broad-reach advertising for an extended period. Sales and share typically decline steadily, faster for small and already-declining brands.
Challenger
A brand competing against a larger incumbent, usually by concentrating on one segment, occasion, or benefit and talking above its size.
Flank
A competitive response that avoids the rival's strongest ground and competes on a different segment, occasion, benefit, or product.
