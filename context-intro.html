Project Context · Intro

Background Before You Diagnose

Why do some brands pour a quarter or more of their revenue into marketing while others spend a few percent? Why does one brand lean on awareness while another lives on performance ads? Seven short pages and two tools to help you answer those questions for your brand and your foil.

01
The Opener

Why Marketing Budgets Differ So Much

Many sources will tell you that brands reinvest 5 to 10% of gross sales or revenue in marketing, and more often 8 to 10%. Gartner's survey of large companies puts the average at 7.7% (Gartner, 2025); The CMO Survey's broader sample puts it at 9.4% (The CMO Survey, 2025); and small-business guidance attributed to the SBA has long suggested 7 to 8% (Houston Chronicle, n.d.). Startups and unknown brands are often cited at 20% or more. Allbirds, fighting its way back, spent 30.8% of revenue on marketing in 2025 (Allbirds, 2025).

The best-known big spender is Red Bull, widely reported to reinvest roughly 25 to 30% of its sales in marketing (Advergize, 2026). Red Bull is private and has never published the number, so treat it as a well-worn estimate, not a disclosure (Cascade, n.d.). That kind of spend is typical of lifestyle brands, where you buy the product partly for the brand, and most of it lands near the top of the funnel. At the other end, a brand that integrates its owned, earned, and shared media well can often cut some of its pure paid advertising, because the unpaid channels carry part of the load.

Trial Discounts: Inside the Budget or On Top of It?

Some brands also invest heavily in first-time trial discounts to get you into the fold. Some count this as part of their 8 to 30% marketing budget. For many, it is an additional sum of money, a "cost of doing business." Part of the reason is accounting: discounts and coupons are usually recorded as a reduction of revenue rather than as marketing expense (Beyond Meat, 2026), so a brand's reported marketing percentage may not include them at all. More on Page 6.

Start with the brand's goal

It is important for a brand to determine its goal, because the goal moves the budget.

  • Growing. A brand trying to grow will often raise its marketing spend.
  • Unknown versus known. Most startups overspend what they hope will become their long-run percentage, just to break through the noise, create awareness, and ultimately convert to sales.
  • Leader or challenger. Well-known brands still lose their luster and need reminding. Brands that stop advertising see sales fall about 16% after one year and 25% after two (Hartnett et al., 2021).
  • Defending. If a competitor is eroding your market share, you may need to raise spend to defend, or pull marketing money from other areas.
  • Entering a new segment. Growing into a new segment often means moving into another brand's territory. Again, that can mean raising spend, or shifting funds toward that segment and pulling from others.
