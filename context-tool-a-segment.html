Project Tool · A

A New Target Is Not Automatically a Good Target

A seven-test scorecard for judging whether a segment is worth targeting. Appendix B runs it on three rows: the brand's current primary segment, its current secondary segment, and one growth opportunity. The growth row is the raw material for Part 3.

01
Why This Tool Exists

The Problem the Scorecard Is Built to Catch

Appendix B asks who buys, why, and whether that group is worth the brand's money, and it asks you to answer with a scorecard rather than a persona. The same scorecard does double duty in Part 3: if your team picks segment expansion, the growth row is your diagnosis, and under any condition it tells you which buyers you are protecting.

The most common failure is not weak creative or media planning. It is assuming a communications change can fix a product, price, or distribution mismatch. "Target [new segment] with [new creative] in [new channel]" is a creative brief written before the segment was diagnosed. Each test below gets a pass, a partial, or a fail, and a recommendation either passes all seven or says which it fails and how it bridges the gap. "It is a great opportunity" is not a bridge.

02
The Scorecard

The Seven Tests

Walk every candidate segment through these in order. Do not skip ahead.

Tip Current segments get five tests; the growth row gets all seven. For the brand's current primary and secondary segments, score Need, Value, Product Fit, Price Fit, and Access. Permission and Conflict are questions about moving somewhere new, so they apply to the growth row. Bring the foil into a row only where its target genuinely differs from yours.

Test 1Need

Does this segment have a meaningful problem or desire the brand could address?

PassReal, articulable friction in the segment's life that current options do not solve well.
FailYou can describe the segment in detail but cannot articulate what they are not getting today.
Prompt"If we built nothing new, what hour of their day is this segment poorly served in?"

Test 2Value

Is the segment large enough, growing enough, or strategically useful enough to justify the work?

PassSizeable now, growing, or strategically important even if small (a learning bet, a defensive bet, a beachhead).
FailSmall, shrinking, and offers no strategic learning value beyond itself.
Prompt"How many real people are in this segment, how much do they spend, and is that number growing?"

Test 3Permission

Would this segment believe this brand if it showed up there?

PassThe brand has known associations the segment would credit or value.
FailThe brand would feel like a non-sequitur, or worse, like a stretch the brand has not earned.
Prompt"If the brand launched in this segment tomorrow with no announcement, would anyone understand why?"

Test 4Product Fit

Can the existing product (or a modest variant) actually satisfy this segment?

PassThe current product addresses the need well, or with light modification.
FailThe product would have to fundamentally change to serve the segment. New tooling, new materials, new format.
Prompt"If a buyer in this segment bought the product as it is today, would they like it enough to buy again?"

Test 5Price Fit

Can this segment afford the brand's price, and does the price say the right thing to them?

PassPrice is within the segment's reach, and the price signal matches what the segment values.
FailPrice is too high (unreachable), too low (signals wrong tier), or misaligned with how the segment shops.
Prompt"Does this segment shop in the brand's tier (discount, value, mainstream, premium, prestige)? See Page 3."

Test 6Access

Can the brand reach and serve this segment through realistic channels?

PassExisting channels touch the segment, or new ones could be added without disrupting the model.
FailThe brand would have to fundamentally rebuild distribution, sales, or service to access them.
Prompt"Where does this segment actually shop, and is the brand there or able to be there? See Page 4."

Test 7Conflict

Would pursuing this segment damage the existing customer base or position?

PassThe new segment can be served without alienating current buyers or diluting the position.
FailGoing after the new segment signals something that makes current buyers feel different about the brand. This is the most easily missed test.
Prompt"Would the brand's most loyal current customer feel anything is different about the brand if they saw the new positioning?"
03
Worked Example

Hoka Into Men's Formal Footwear

Hoka built a successful athletic brand around maximum-cushion running shoes. Could it move into men's formal footwear? The results below are illustrative, not a real strategy recommendation.

TestScoreWhy
1 · NeedPassDress shoes are notoriously uncomfortable, and Cole Haan built a business on athletic comfort inside formal designs.
2 · ValuePassA multi-billion-dollar category, mature but stable among professionals.
3 · PermissionPartialStrong comfort permission, no formal permission. Hoka can stretch along its core meaning, not against its athletic look.
4 · Product FitFailDifferent lasts, leather construction, soles, and finishing. A new product line, not a modification.
5 · Price FitPartialHoka sits at $140–200; premium dress starts near $300. Possible at the low premium end, carefully.
6 · AccessPartial failRunning stores, REI, and Dick's don't reach this buyer. It would need Nordstrom-type partners and new DTC merchandising.
7 · ConflictRiskDilutes the serious-performance signal. Even On, which stretched into lifestyle, has stopped short of formal (see the Hoka/On page).
Diagnosis

Two passes, two partials, one partial fail, one fail, one risk. The move is not impossible, but it cannot be executed through communications alone. It would need new product, new wholesale partners, careful pricing, and probably a sub-brand rather than the core Hoka name. The communications work is the last 10% of this move, not the whole move.

The teaching point is the structure. A recommendation that names which tests it passes, which it does not, and how it bridges the gaps is a serious recommendation. One that calls the segment "a great opportunity" without this work is not.

04
How to Use It

What to Do with the Scorecard

For the growth row, the scorecard produces one of three outcomes:

  • Seven clean passes. Rare. If you genuinely got there, defend the diagnosis in a sentence and move on to the creative brief.
  • One or two partial failures. The most common and most useful outcome. Name them and explain how Part 3 bridges them (a product variant, a price tier, a sub-brand, a new channel partner).
  • Three or more partial or full failures. Wrong segment. Pick another rather than defending a recommendation against four problems at once.

The scorecard is for diagnosis, not justification. If you find yourself adjusting the criteria to make a favorite segment "pass," you are using the tool backwards.

In Your Project

The scorecard is the spine of Appendix B, and Appendix B hands Part 3 its raw material.

  • Build Appendix B as three rows. Current primary, current secondary, growth opportunity. Five tests for the current rows, all seven for the growth row, with a one-line reason per score. A grid fits on one page; prose does not.
  • Show your evidence per row. Reviews, comment patterns, follower data, retailer placement, who appears in the ads. Label each with its level on the Evidence Ladder.
  • Test two growth candidates before committing. The first segment you think of is rarely the strongest. Show only the winner in the appendix.
  • Use it under every condition. Segment expansion builds on the growth row. A budget cut uses the current rows to decide whom to protect. A competitor shock asks which of your rows the foil is chasing.

Research Prompts

Short on time? Start with the Google searches, then use the source list to go deeper. Use the Think It Through prompts only after you have evidence in hand.

Quick Google Searches

  • "[brand] customer demographics" · "[brand] target audience"
  • "[brand] reviews reddit" · "[brand] vs [foil] reddit"
  • "[category] consumer trends 2026" · "[segment] [category] survey" (e.g., "Gen Z energy drink survey")
  • "[brand] where to buy" · "[brand] retailers list"

Find the Facts

  • Segment sizing: Statista, Mintel, and IBISWorld through the library, census data, industry-association reports
  • Who actually buys: product reviews (Amazon, the brand site, Reddit), comment sections, follower demographics, who appears in the ads
  • Brand permission: reviews and social comments, plus brand trackers where available (YouGov BrandIndex, Morning Consult)
  • Price and access: retailer site walks, the brand's "where to buy" page, retailer locators

Think It Through

  • "Score [brand]'s current primary segment, current secondary segment, and [growth segment] on the scorecard: five tests for the current rows, seven for the growth row. Give one sentence of reasoning per score."
  • "Which one or two tests are the binding constraints on [growth segment], and what would have to change for them to pass?"

Vocabulary

Glossary

Growth segment
A new or adjacent group of buyers the brand could realistically grow into. The third row of Appendix B, and the starting point for Part 3 if your team chooses segment expansion.
Brand permission
The set of meanings and associations a brand carries that determine which extensions feel credible to buyers. A brand can stretch along the dimension of its core meaning, but stretching against it usually fails.
Brand stretch
The act of extending a brand into a new product category, segment, or use case. Stretches succeed when permission holds and product, price, and channel can follow. Failures are usually rooted in one of those four.
Sub-brand
A distinct brand name (or modifier) used when the core brand cannot credibly enter a new segment but the parent company still wants to. Toyota's Lexus, Gap's Old Navy, P&G's Olay are all examples.
Binding constraint
The single test or condition that decides whether a segment move can work, holding everything else equal. The first thing to identify when a scorecard shows several partial fails.
