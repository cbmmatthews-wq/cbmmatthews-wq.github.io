Project Tool · B

The Evidence Ladder

Five levels of evidence for every claim in your report and appendices. The handout asks you to cite external numbers this way. The point of the ladder is not to find perfect data. The point is to label honestly which rung your evidence actually sits on.

01
Why This Tool Exists

The Honest Number Beats the Confident One

There is a specific kind of sentence that turns up in nearly every student report:

"The brand spends 70% on brand and 30% on performance."

The sentence is precise. The sentence is confident. The sentence is, in nine cases out of ten, completely made up. Real brands rarely disclose this split. Public companies disclose it in aggregated form at best. Private companies disclose nothing.

The problem is not that the student is lying. The problem is that the report does not distinguish between things the student knows, things the student can infer, and things the student is guessing. A grader reading the report cannot tell which is which. The result reads as overconfident at best and fabricated at worst.

The evidence ladder fixes this with a single discipline: every consequential claim in the report gets labeled with the level of evidence behind it. Five levels. The label takes one word. The intellectual honesty it produces is the difference between a report that gets returned with edits and a report that gets graded.

02
The Five Levels

The Five Rungs of Evidence

Levels 1 through 4 are claims the report can defend. Level 5 is the honest acknowledgment that the report cannot. All five are acceptable in your project, as long as the label is correct.

1Strongest

Disclosed Facts

Statements made by the brand itself in materials a reader could verify directly.

ExamplesAnnual reports and 10-K filings (SEC EDGAR), 10-Q quarterly reports, investor presentations, press releases, official company statements, regulatory filings, retailer counts on the brand's site, store-locator data.

Label as"According to [Brand]'s 2024 10-K..." or "The company disclosed in its Q3 earnings call that..."

2Strong

Directly Observable Evidence

Things any reader could see for themselves with public tools, without needing the brand to confirm.

ExamplesMeta Ad Library (active and historical ads), TikTok Creative Center, Google Ads Transparency Center, the brand's social-media activity, retailer presence (in-store visits, retailer search), website promotions, email blasts (sign up for the list), packaging, sponsorships visible in public, physical distribution counts.

Label as"Observed in Meta Ad Library between January and March 2026..." or "A retailer audit of three major grocery chains in February 2026 found..."

3Moderate

Credible Outside Estimates

Numbers and analyses published by third parties with known methodology, used widely enough to be checkable.

ExamplesTrade publications (Modern Retail, Marketing Brew, AdAge, Business of Fashion), market-research firms (NielsenIQ, IRI, Statista, eMarketer, Gartner, Bain), agency case studies, media-monitoring services, well-sourced journalism, financial-analyst reports.

Label as"eMarketer estimated in May 2025 that..." or "Per a 2024 Bain & Altagamma study..."

4Weaker

Informed Inference

Reasoned conclusions drawn from Level 1, 2, or 3 evidence, clearly flagged as the reader's own interpretation.

Examples"Given that [brand]'s observable ad spend in Meta Ad Library skews heavily to brand-style creative..."; "Given the brand's wholesale-heavy channel mix, we can infer that customer-acquisition cost..."; "The pattern of seasonal pricing suggests..."

Label as"This appears to lean toward...", "Based on observable behavior, we estimate...", "The available evidence suggests..."

5Honest

Acknowledged Unknown

A specific claim the report cannot defend at any of the four levels above. Naming the unknown is itself a contribution; pretending it is known is the failure.

ExamplesThe actual brand-versus-performance budget split at a private DTC company. The exact CAC at a brand with no disclosed financials. The actual incrementality of a competitor's trade promotion. Specific media-mix percentages for any private company.

Label as"The actual percentage split is not publicly disclosed. Any specific number would be speculation."

03
Worked Example

Walking a Liquid Death Claim Through All Five Levels

A student wants to write: "Liquid Death spends 75% of its budget on brand-building and 25% on performance." Specific, confident, and unsupported. Here is what each rung can and cannot carry.

LevelWhat's availableSupports the 75/25 claim?
1 · DisclosedLiquid Death is private. Funding-round revenue figures exist; media-mix figures do not.No
2 · ObservableMeta Ad Library, TikTok and YouTube activity, retailer presence, celebrity and stunt activations. Shows what the brand does, not what it spends.No, but it feeds Level 4
3 · Outside estimateSacra revenue estimates; AdAge and Marketing Brew coverage of its stunt-driven strategy.Supports "stunt-driven," not a percentage
4 · InferenceCombining 2 and 3: the brand's communications appear to skew heavily toward brand-building.Yes, as a labeled estimate
5 · UnknownThe actual split is not disclosed and cannot be known from outside.Must be said
The Rewrite

Before: "Liquid Death spends 75% on brand and 25% on performance."

After: "Liquid Death's communications appear to skew heavily toward brand-building based on observable activity in Meta Ad Library and the prominence of stunt and celebrity-led marketing covered in AdAge and Sacra. The actual budget split is not publicly disclosed. We estimate roughly 70-75% brand to 25-30% performance, with the caveat that this is an inference (Level 4) rather than a sourced figure (Level 1 or 3)."

The number is the same. The defense is now visible. This is what intellectual honesty in a report looks like.

04
How to Use It

Applying the Ladder in Your Report

Five claim types in this project almost always require the ladder. They are the places where students reach for confident numbers without the evidence to support them.

  • PESO mix. Specific percentages by quadrant are almost always Level 4 or 5. Observable activity in each quadrant is Level 2. Document what you see, then estimate.
  • Brand versus performance / 60/40 split. Same problem. Honest version: name the inputs you can see and the inference you are drawing.
  • Ad spend estimate. Public companies disclose this at Level 1. Private companies do not. If the brand is private, you are working at Level 4 plus Level 5 acknowledgment. A figure repeated in many articles is still not a disclosure: Red Bull's often-quoted 25–30% of revenue is a widely reported estimate (Level 3 at best), because Red Bull has never published it.
  • Distribution economics. Channel share is sometimes disclosed (Level 1), often estimated by trade press (Level 3), often inferred from observable retailer footprint (Level 4). Be clear about which.
  • Competitor strategy. What a competitor does is observable (Level 2). What a competitor intends is inference (Level 4) unless they have stated it publicly (Level 1).

A report that confidently asserts unknowable numbers is weaker than a report that estimates them honestly and says so.

In Your Project

The ladder is a labeling discipline. Apply it to every major numerical or strategic claim, not just the easy ones.

  • Start with the appendices. The appendices are read first, and they carry the numbers. Every external figure there should carry its level.
  • Label every consequential claim. "Level 1 disclosure" or "Level 4 inference" or "Level 5 unknown" can be a footnote, a parenthetical, or a short phrase in the body. The form does not matter; the discipline does.
  • Don't fake Level 1. Inventing a precise percentage and citing "industry sources" without naming them is the move the ladder is built to catch. If you cannot name the source, the claim is at Level 4 at best.
  • Use Level 5 deliberately. Naming what is genuinely unknown shows the grader you understand the limits of your evidence. It also points to where additional research would change the recommendation.
  • Be especially careful with competitor claims. Most "[competitor] spends X" claims in student reports are Level 4 dressed as Level 1. Audit them ruthlessly.

Research Prompts

Short on time? Start with the Google searches; each one tends to land on a particular rung. Then use the source list to go deeper.

Quick Google Searches

  • Level 1: "[brand] 10-K" · "[brand] investor presentation" · "[brand] annual report advertising expense"
  • Level 2: "[brand] Meta Ad Library" · "[brand] TikTok Creative Center"
  • Level 3: "[brand] revenue" · "[brand] marketing strategy AdAge" · "[brand] Sacra" (for private companies)

Find the Facts (by Level)

  • Level 1: SEC EDGAR (sec.gov/edgar), annual reports, the brand's investor-relations page and newsroom
  • Level 2: Meta Ad Library (facebook.com/ads/library), Google Ads Transparency Center, TikTok Creative Center, the brand's social accounts and email list, in-store audits, the "where to buy" page
  • Level 3: Modern Retail, Marketing Brew, AdAge, Business of Fashion, NielsenIQ, Circana, Statista, eMarketer, Gartner, Sacra
  • Level 4: Your own reasoning, clearly flagged. AI assistants are useful for stress-testing the inference, not for inventing the data it rests on
  • Level 5: The honest acknowledgment. No source needed

Think It Through

  • "For each major claim in our report and appendices, name the level of evidence behind it."
  • "Identify any claim asserted as fact (Level 1) that is really an inference (Level 4). Rewrite it with the label."

Vocabulary

Glossary

Disclosed fact
Information stated by the brand itself in a material a reader can verify directly: SEC filing, annual report, press release, earnings transcript. The strongest evidence available for any claim about a public company.
Observable evidence
Information any reader can confirm through public tools, without the brand's cooperation: ad libraries, social-media activity, retailer audits, packaging, email lists, sponsorship visibility.
Credible outside estimate
A figure or analysis published by a third party with known methodology and adequate transparency: trade press, market-research firms, agency case studies, financial analysts.
Informed inference
A reasoned conclusion drawn from Level 1, 2, or 3 evidence, with the reasoning visible to the reader. Acceptable if labeled; problematic if asserted as fact.
Acknowledged unknown
The deliberate naming of a claim the report cannot defend at any of the four upper levels. The honest fifth-level move. Not a weakness in the report; a sign that the writer understands the limits of the evidence.
Labeling discipline
The practice of tagging every consequential claim with its evidence level. The label is the contribution: it shows the reader what is known, what is inferred, and what is acknowledged as unknown.
