When It Goes Wrong: Crises and Rumors

Every brand eventually gets bad news it didn't plan. The cases below show that the event rarely decides the outcome. The response does.

The Cases

What Reactive PR Is Up Against

This page expands the crisis overview in PR Basics. Andrews calls this reactive marketing PR: PR undertaken because of outside pressure rather than opportunity. Its most dramatic causes are product defects and failures, but the list below shows how varied the triggers are.

CaseWhat HappenedThe Cost
VWSoftware that let diesel cars cheat emissions testsAbout $15 billion U.S. settlement
BPDeepwater Horizon blowout, 2010; 11 workers killed, months of oil in the GulfMore than $18.7 billion in fines; fell off Interbrand's top 100
SamsungGalaxy Note 7 batteries catching fire; full recallAbout $4 billion in recall costs and lost sales
ChipotleE. coli, norovirus, and salmonella outbreaks in 2015Stock down about a third
Wells FargoNearly 2 million accounts opened without customer authorizationFines, lawsuits, CEO resignation
SubwayLongtime spokesperson Jared Fogle convicted of child-pornography chargesEnded a relationship dating to 1988
KFC / Taco BellTV footage of rats running through a New York store; more than 1,000 blogs picked it upDamage to every store, not just the one

The KFC case makes a point the others don't: no crisis stays local anymore. One store's problem becomes every store's problem once the footage spreads, because customers generalize from one vivid image to the whole brand.

02
Three Responses

Own It, Fumble It, Disprove It

Own it · Tylenol, 1982
Seven deaths from cyanide-laced capsules in Chicago
  • Pulled the product from shelves nationwide, immediately
  • Spokespeople on TV warning consumers not to take it
  • Introduced tamper-proof packaging that became the industry standard
  • Free replacements for product people had thrown away
Pundits doubted anything could be sold under the Tylenol name again. It regained its share soon after relaunch.
Fumble it · Perrier, 1990
Traces of benzene found in bottles
  • Offered several shifting explanations
  • Recalled reluctantly (72 million U.S. bottles), then worldwide
  • Global recall cost more than $150 million
U.S. sales fell 40%. Evian became the leading import. Perrier never fully recovered.

The Tylenol playbook got a second test in 1991, when two people in Washington State died from cyanide-laced Sudafed. Its maker copied J&J step for step (pulled the product, suspended advertising, opened a toll-free line, offered a $100,000 reward) and saw only a brief sales slump.

Disprove it: the Pepsi syringe hoax

In 1993 a man in New Orleans told CNN he had found a syringe in a can of Diet Pepsi, and copycat reports followed around the country. PepsiCo knew the claim was nearly impossible: cans are open for less than a second on the filling line. So it showed people. It released video of the bottling process, seen by an estimated 187 million viewers. Its CEO went on Nightline beside the FDA commissioner, who warned of penalties for false reports. Then a store camera caught a woman putting a syringe into a can, and that footage aired too. Sales were back to normal within weeks.

When the problem is real, act fast and own it. When it isn't, act fast and prove it.
The lesson of Tylenol and Pepsi
03
The Playbook

What to Do in the First 72 Hours

72 hours
The response window
In 128 incidents where a celebrity endorser created bad publicity, firms responding within 72 hours saw about a 2% rise in stock value. Each day after that was worse, but responding late still beat not responding (Belch).
Loyalty
Is the buffer
Customers who already like a company challenge negative publicity; less loyal customers absorb it (Andrews).

After the Samsung recall, crisis experts reduced the response to six steps (Andrews, Ch. 21):

  1. Be open with consumers, media, regulators, and shareholders.
  2. Follow through on promises and report progress.
  3. Halt marketing campaigns until the crisis is resolved.
  4. If you must advertise, do it carefully, including for related products.
  5. Put a human face on the company.
  6. Be the first with the news.

Why are companies so often slow? One crisis expert quoted by Andrews blames instinct. Leadership's first worry is the stock price, the management team, or their own bonuses. The last thing they think about is what the parent with two kids in the shopping cart now thinks of the product. The playbook is easy; overriding that instinct is the hard part.

Some brands don't recover, and change their names instead. Belch's examples: Philip Morris became Altria, Blackwater became Xe and then Academi, and the Lance Armstrong Foundation became Livestrong. A name change can create distance. It can't create a different history.

Smaller crises: online reviews

Most crises are small: a one-star review that's getting attention. Andrews' advice for responding: read it closely and address the specific issues (no canned reply), apologize if any part of it is true, don't get defensive, respond quickly, invite suggestions, and leave a name and a way to continue the conversation offline.

04
Rumors

Fighting Something That Isn't True

A commercial rumor is a widely circulated but unverified claim about a product, brand, company, or store. Rumors are probably the hardest problem PR faces: they spread fast, almost always say something repulsive, and are almost always false. Andrews sorts them into two kinds.

Conspiracy rumors
"The company secretly supports something awful"
  • P&G's moon-and-stars logo as a satanic symbol. P&G eventually redesigned the logo.
  • Popeyes' founder supporting a politician with Klan ties. He called a press conference, offered a $25,000 reward for the source, and the rumor died.
Contamination rumors
"There's something disgusting in it"
  • Corona "contaminated with urine," started by a rival distributor. Sales fell 80% in some markets until a court-ordered public retraction.
  • Worms in fast-food burgers; spider eggs in bubble gum; KFC's "eight-legged chickens" in China, which ended in a court-ordered apology and fines.

Silence or response?

Some companies stay quiet, fearing that a denial will spread the rumor further. The expert view in Andrews is the opposite: rumors are like fires, and time is the worst enemy. The process is to stay alert (where is it, who's saying it), assess the damage (sales, morale, image), and then run a counter-campaign that:

  1. picks the specific claims to refute,
  2. says plainly that they're untrue and unfair,
  3. uses the channels where the rumor is spreading, and
  4. puts the message in the mouth of a credible spokesperson who isn't the company: a scientist, a regulator, a respected civic leader.

When refuting doesn't work

The burger-worm problem

Some rumors are so vivid that a denial just repeats the image. Research Andrews cites found that for rumors like the McDonald's worm story, simple refutation was less effective than two indirect strategies. Positive storage gives people something good to file next to the rumor (a delicious dish made with worms, in the original study). Positive retrieval asks people to recall their own good experiences, such as the McDonald's they visit most often.

The principle: you often can't erase a vivid association, but you can outweigh it.

Don't just deny the image. Give them a better one.
05
Practice

Scenario

You own a restaurant near campus. A false rumor is spreading on group chats and TikTok that your head chef has a contagious disease. Weekday traffic is down 30%. What do you do this week?
One defensible answer
  • Don't wait. Silence reads as confirmation.
  • Refute the specific claim, not "rumors" in general.
  • Borrow credibility. A clean health inspection or a local health official's statement beats anything the owner says.
  • Use the channels where it spread. A press release won't reach a group chat; a short video from the kitchen might.
  • Put a face on it. The owner and the chef, on camera, cooking.
  • Prompt positive retrieval. Invite regulars back to remind them of their own experience.
Takeaway
  • No crisis stays local. One vivid image gets generalized to the whole brand.
  • Real problem: act fast and own it (Tylenol). False claim: act fast and prove it (Pepsi). Hesitating and shifting stories is how brands die (Perrier).
  • The first 72 hours matter most, and loyalty built beforehand is the buffer.
  • Rumors are conspiracy or contamination. Fight them quickly, specifically, and through a credible third party.
  • When denial just repeats the image, outweigh it with positive storage or retrieval.
