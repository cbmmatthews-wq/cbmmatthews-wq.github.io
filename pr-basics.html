Public Relations, Publicity & Earned Media

Every other tool in the promotional mix talks to customers directly. PR has to convince someone else to do the talking. That one fact explains almost everything good and bad about it.

Overview

What This Page Does

PR usually shows up in two places in marketing frameworks. In the PESO model it is the core of Earned media: high credibility, low control. In the touch-points framework it lives mostly in the Unexpected category, the one the company controls least and the customer believes most. This page goes inside that box.

The textbooks treat PR as a grab bag: press releases, crisis management, sponsorships, corporate image ads, cause marketing, community relations. They're all here. But a single idea ties them together, and it's worth stating up front:

PR is the only promotional tool that has to persuade a gatekeeper before it can persuade a customer.
The thesis of this page

An ad runs because you paid for it. A coupon goes out because you mailed it. A news story, a podcast mention, a reviewer's verdict, or an AI assistant's citation happens only because someone who doesn't work for you decided it was worth their audience's time. That gatekeeper is where PR's credibility comes from. It is also why PR is slow, unpredictable, and impossible to buy on demand.

The page draws mainly on Belch & Belch (Ch. 17), with shorter pieces from Andrews & Shimp (Ch. 21) where they cover ground Belch doesn't: content marketing, rumors, word of mouth, and ambush marketing. Two topics come from outside both books: why the industry's favorite PR metric is discredited, and what AI search is doing to the value of earned media.

01
Definitions

What PR Is, and How It Differs from Publicity

The traditional definition, from the trade newsletter Public Relations News, describes PR as a management function with three jobs: figure out what the public thinks of you, line up your policies with the public interest, and then run a program of action and communication to "earn public understanding and acceptance" (Belch, Ch. 17). Notice the order. Listening comes first and communicating comes last. Notice too that customers are only one of the publics in view. Employees, investors, neighbors, regulators, and activists all count.

That broad definition is what Andrews calls general PR. The narrower slice aimed at actual and prospective customers is marketing public relations, or MPR, a term coined by Thomas Harris (Andrews, Ch. 21). Most of this page is about MPR, but the general-PR publics matter because they can sink a marketing program. As one editor quoted by Belch put it (paraphrasing), it's hard to sell an eco-friendly detergent while your company is in the news for dumping chemicals in a river.

The distinction between PR and publicity matters more than it looks. PR is something you do. Publicity is something that happens to you, and it can be good or bad. A well-run PR department spends much of its time trying to turn the second into the first.

1.2 Where PR Sits in the Organization

Belch describes a continuum. At one end, PR is a non-marketing function: a separate department, often a separate agency, whose job is keeping peace with all publics, with customers just one group among many. At the other end, PR reports to marketing and every relationship is judged by what it does for sales. Belch's preferred position is the middle: strong marketing and strong PR departments that work together without either absorbing the other. Make PR too subordinate and it loses the independence that makes journalists and communities trust it.

1.3 PR as a Process, and Its Publics

Because PR is a management function, it gets planned like one. Cutlip, Center, and Broom's four steps are the standard: (1) define the problem, (2) plan and program, (3) act and communicate, (4) evaluate. Step 1 is where attitude research happens, and Belch gives four reasons to do it: it feeds the plan, it works as an early-warning system, it wins internal support for fixing a problem, and it makes the eventual messaging sharper.

Then come the publics. One classification surprises students: John Marston counts community members and current customers as internal publics, alongside employees and investors, because the organization already deals with them in the ordinary course of business.

  • Internal publics (already connected): employees, investors, the community, suppliers, and current customers. Reached through newsletters, annual reports, impact reports, and day-to-day contact.
  • External publics (no standing connection): the media and educators, both gatekeepers; civic groups; the financial community; and government, where one regulation can be worth more than a year of advertising.
Takeaway
  • PR is a management function: listen first, align policy, then communicate. Customers are one public among many.
  • MPR is the customer-facing slice. Most marketing conversations about "PR" mean MPR.
  • PR is something you do. Publicity is something that happens to you, positive or negative.
  • Corporate advertising is paid, so it isn't earned, but it lives in the PR family because it sells the organization rather than a product.
  • PR and marketing work best as partners. Fold PR entirely into marketing and it loses the independence that makes it credible.
02
The Core Trade-Off

Credibility vs. Control

Every argument for PR and every argument against it comes back to the same trade. You give up control over the message in exchange for a source the audience believes. The touch-points framework shows the pattern: the less a company controls a touch point, the more it tends to move customers. PR is that pattern turned into a discipline.

What PR buys you
Advantages
  • Credibility. No visible sponsor, so audiences treat it as news, not persuasion.
  • Cost. Often the most affordable option for small companies.
  • Clutter avoidance. A news story doesn't sit in an ad break.
  • Lead generation. Coverage of a breakthrough produces inquiries.
  • Niche reach. Trade press and specialist outlets reach groups too small to advertise to.
  • Image as insurance. A strong reputation helps a company ride out the next crisis.
  • Staying power. A good article can be remembered for years; a social post is often a flash in the pan.
What PR costs you
Disadvantages
  • No control over whether it runs. Space and time are never guaranteed.
  • No control over timing. It may run too early, too late, or never.
  • No control over accuracy. The story you pitched and the story that ran may barely resemble each other.
  • The attribution gap. Audiences may never connect the story back to the brand.
  • Hard to tie in slogans or other advertising devices.
  • Coordination risk. PR running separately from marketing produces mixed messages.
  • No standard effectiveness measure (see Section 6).

2.1 Why Publicity Hits So Hard

Belch gives three reasons publicity can outweigh every other form of promotion combined. First, it's credible, because it isn't seen as company-sponsored (negative publicity never is). Second, it seems endorsed by the outlet that runs it: a Car and Driver award reflects the magazine's judgment, not the automaker's. Third, it's news, and people like passing news along. That last point is where publicity turns into word of mouth.

The upside examples are striking. Cabernet Sauvignon sales rose an average of 45% in the month after a 60 Minutes report linking moderate red wine consumption to heart health. Fortnite became a sensation with essentially no advertising spend. The downside examples are just as large, and Section 4 covers them.

2.2 The "Rise of PR" Argument and Its Limits

In 2002, Al and Laura Ries published The Fall of Advertising and the Rise of PR, arguing that new brands should launch on publicity and use advertising only to maintain them once they're established. They pointed to eBay, Starbucks, PlayStation, and The Body Shop. Belch adds Tesla, Apple, and Gillette, which uses PR as the lead medium in every launch.

Andrews' two pushbacks

1. Most products aren't news. A slightly better detergent or a new flavor extension won't get free coverage, because editors don't care. Launch-by-PR works for products that are genuinely unique or visible.

2. Newsworthiness wears off. Even a truly novel product stops being a story after launch. At that point, keeping the brand's equity up falls to advertising.

Both pushbacks point to the same practical test, which the Earning Earned reading calls the remove-the-name test: would this story still interest anyone if you took the company's name out of the headline? If not, it isn't a PR opportunity. It's an ad, and should be bought as one.

Borrowed Voices

Earned third parties, paid third parties, and why the difference matters

PR works because someone else is saying it. That's a source effect, the same idea behind celebrity endorsement. Both strategies borrow credibility from a voice that isn't the brand's own. They differ in one crucial way: the audience knows the celebrity was paid.

That difference predicts a lot. An unpaid reviewer, journalist, or fan has independence, and independence is what makes the message believable. A paid celebrity brings attention and image transfer but not independence, which helps explain why pooled research finds celebrity endorsements average out near zero effect, and why celebrities underperform quality seals and awards. A J.D. Power award, which State Farm advertises, is the third-party voice working as intended.

There's a middle case. Andrews notes research finding that when a word-of-mouth agent discloses their connection to a brand, it doesn't hurt their credibility, and actually increases how often the message gets passed along. Disclosure isn't the problem. Hiding the connection is.

For a deeper look at when paid voices do and don't work, see Do Celebrities Actually Sell Anything?

Independence is the asset. Payment spends it.
Takeaway
  • PR trades control for credibility. Every advantage and disadvantage traces back to that trade.
  • Publicity is powerful because it's credible, appears endorsed by the outlet, and is news people repeat.
  • "Launch on PR, maintain with ads" works for genuinely newsworthy products, and only while they stay newsworthy.
  • Remove-the-name test: if the story dies without the brand name, buy an ad instead.
  • Earned and paid third parties both borrow credibility. Only the earned one keeps its independence.

Offense

Proactive PR: The Toolkit

Andrews splits MPR into two modes. Proactive MPR is offensive and opportunity-seeking: launches, announcements, content, buzz. Reactive MPR is defensive: responding to crises, rumors, and attacks. This section covers offense. Section 4 covers defense.

3.2 What MPR Can Do for a Brand

Harris's list of the jobs marketing PR can do is the most useful part of Belch's chapter. It shows PR doing things advertising can't.

Build excitement before ads break

Apple builds anticipation through coverage before launch, then lets lines outside stores become the story.

Launch with little or no advertising

Tesla, Segway, Crayola. Works only when the product is genuinely news (see Andrews' pushback).

Make the ad itself the news

Super Bowl spots get covered, ranked, and shared, so the ad earns far more exposure than its media buy.

Add a service

Butterball's turkey hotline took 25,000 calls its first holiday season. Helpful, branded, and newsworthy.

Build brand–customer bonds

The Pillsbury Bake-Off has run since 1949. Participation creates loyalty no ad can match.

Defend a product at risk

After the auto bailout made private jets a symbol of excess, Cessna's "Rise" campaign made the business case for them.

Give a reason to buy

Energizer's "change your smoke-alarm battery when you change your clocks" built goodwill and battery sales.

Influence the influentials

Get the story to the opinion leaders who brief everyone else (more in 3.4).

One example in Belch shows how far modern PR has drifted toward performance marketing. WestJet, much smaller than Air Canada, turned PR stunts into videos (an April Fools' "raise your hands for aerodynamics" gag, surprise holiday gifts), then retargeted the viewers with ticket ads. The airline reported millions of dollars in flights bought by people who had just watched the videos. Earned attention, harvested with paid media: a PESO flywheel in one campaign.

3.3 Content Marketing and Native Advertising

Andrews treats content marketing as the fastest-growing area of proactive PR: producing, curating, and sharing brand content built around what customers need and that delivers visible value. Belch's best example is Betty Crocker. Search data showed that mothers looking for help baking birthday cakes searched for "birthday cake," not brand names, so Betty Crocker produced instructional videos that barely mentioned the brand. The videos drove more than 70 million visitors to its site.

Its controversial cousin is native advertising: brand-sponsored content made to blend in with the editorial content around it. Defenders say it delivers useful information people actually want to read. Critics say looking like journalism is the point, and that makes it deceptive. The FTC held a workshop on the issue in 2013 and in 2015 issued an enforcement policy statement on deceptively formatted advertising: if a reasonable reader can't tell it's an ad, it needs a clearer label.

Is native advertising PR or advertising?

A question that comes up every semester

In PESO terms, it's Paid. The brand buys the placement. What makes it feel like PR is that it borrows the look of earned media, which is exactly why regulators care about disclosure.

Content marketing on the brand's own site (Betty Crocker's videos, Coca-Cola's Journey site) is Owned. It becomes Earned only when an independent outlet or person picks it up and passes it along by choice.

Ask who paid for the placement, not what it looks like.

3.4 Word of Mouth and Buzz, Briefly

Publicity becomes powerful when people repeat it. Andrews defines word of mouth (WOM) as informal communication among consumers about products, and the average American has more than 120 such conversations a week. Three ideas are worth keeping:

  • The two-step flow. Advertising and publicity start the conversation; opinion leaders and social networks carry it. Neither works as well alone.
  • Gladwell's three rules: the law of the few (a handful of connected people start it), stickiness (the message has to be memorable), and the power of context (the moment has to be right).
  • Emotion drives sharing. High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) spread content; low-arousal ones (sadness) don't.

McKinsey turns this into tactics: make the product unique or visible, seed the early adopters others copy, and ration supply, because people talk about what they can't have yet.

Go deeper

Andrews' viral examples (Blendtec, Old Spice, Oprah's Pontiac giveaway) are a decade or more old. For a current treatment of why people share, including Berger's STEPPS framework and 2024 survey data on why people pass along brand posts, see What Makes Content Shareable?

Takeaway
  • Proactive MPR seeks opportunities; reactive MPR solves problems.
  • Executive-statement releases land in the news section and carry more credibility than product releases.
  • MPR does jobs ads can't: pre-launch buzz, turning ads into news, defending products at risk, building community.
  • Content marketing on your own site is Owned; native advertising is Paid. Neither is Earned until someone independent passes it on.
  • The Dell example shows the sequence: earn first, then pay to extend.
  • Publicity becomes word of mouth through connected people, sticky messages, and high-arousal emotion.
04
Defense

Reactive PR: Crises and Rumors

Belch opens his chapter with the costs of bad publicity. Kanye West's antisemitic remarks in 2022 ended his deals with adidas, the Gap, and Balenciaga; adidas estimated that ending the Yeezy line would cost it about a quarter of a billion dollars that year. Andrews adds a run of recent disasters: VW's emissions cheating, Chipotle's outbreaks, Samsung's exploding Note 7 batteries, Wells Fargo's unauthorized accounts. Belch's other opening story is the most famous, and more instructive than the textbook realizes.

Did Ronaldo really cost Coca-Cola $4 billion?

A publicity story that became "fact," including in Belch

The claim went around the world in 2021: Coke's shares fell 1.6% soon after Ronaldo pushed two bottles aside at a press conference, wiping about $4 billion off its market value. Fact-checkers then pointed out that the stock was already falling before the press conference began, and that the day was Coca-Cola's ex-dividend date, when a drop of roughly that size is expected (Snopes; City A.M.).

That makes it a perfect PR lesson. A vivid story with a big number spread faster than the correction and ended up in a textbook. The gesture was real. The causal claim wasn't.

Vivid beats accurate, until someone checks.

4.1 What Separates a Survivable Crisis from a Fatal One

Tylenol, 1982
Seven deaths from cyanide-laced capsules

Pulled the product nationwide, immediately; told consumers on TV not to take it; introduced tamper-proof packaging; replaced product people had thrown away.

Analysts predicted the brand was dead. It regained its share soon after relaunch.
Perrier, 1990
Traces of benzene in bottles

Offered shifting explanations, recalled reluctantly, then had to recall worldwide at a cost of more than $150 million.

U.S. sales fell 40%. Perrier never fully recovered.

The difference wasn't the severity of the problem. It was speed and candor. Two more findings sharpen the point. In a study of 128 incidents where a celebrity endorser created bad publicity, firms that responded within 72 hours actually saw their stock rise slightly, and every day after that made things worse (Belch). And loyal customers push back against negative publicity, while less loyal ones absorb it (Andrews): reputation built in good times gets spent in bad ones. The crisis playbook that follows from this is short: be open, be first with the news, put a human face on the company, pause the advertising, and follow through on every promise.

4.2 Rumors

A commercial rumor is a widely circulated, unverified, and usually false claim about a product or company. Andrews sorts them into two kinds. Conspiracy rumors say the company secretly supports something offensive (P&G's logo as a satanic symbol). Contamination rumors say there's something disgusting in the product (Corona and urine, a rumor started by a rival distributor that cut sales 80% in some markets). Saying nothing feels safe but usually isn't. The expert advice is to treat a rumor like a fire: refute the specific claim quickly, through the channels where it's spreading, using a credible spokesperson who isn't the company.

Is any publicity good publicity?

Both textbooks ask. The research answer: it depends on whether anyone knows you.

A study of New York Times book reviews found a negative review raised sales for unknown authors by about 45%, because being reviewed at all built awareness, while it cut sales for established authors by about 15% (Berger, Sorensen & Rasmussen, 2010). That's the awareness threshold at work. Below it, almost any attention helps. Above it, people already know you, and bad press just makes them think less of you.

Attention helps the unknown. Reputation is what the known have to lose.
Go deeper

For the full set of cases (the Pepsi syringe hoax, Samsung, VW, Wells Fargo), the six-step crisis playbook, how to run an anti-rumor campaign and when refutation backfires, and a campus restaurant scenario to work through, see When It Goes Wrong: Crises and Rumors.

Takeaway
  • Negative publicity can be very costly, but check the number. Vivid claims spread faster than corrections.
  • Tylenol vs. Perrier: speed and candor save brands; hesitation and shifting stories sink them.
  • Respond within about 72 hours. Loyalty built beforehand is the buffer.
  • Rumors are conspiracy or contamination. Fight them fast, with a credible third-party voice.
  • "Any publicity is good publicity" holds only for brands nobody has heard of yet.
05
Paying to Be Associated

Corporate Advertising, Sponsorship, and Cause

The last part of the PR family involves paying, not for a product message, but to put the organization next to something people already value: an idea, an event, a cause. Belch groups these under corporate advertising, which promotes the firm rather than any product.

5.1 Corporate Advertising and Why It's Controversial

Belch lists four common criticisms: consumers aren't interested in it, it's executive self-indulgence (top managers often dictate the copy), it's a sign the company is in trouble, and it's a waste of money because it asks for nothing specific. Its defense is that it positions the firm (compare what comes to mind for Apple versus ConAgra), says what PR couldn't get the media to cover, and reaches a select audience, often investors and business leaders rather than the general public. It comes in four main forms:

Image advertising

General positioning (Accenture), program sponsorship (Antiques Roadshow on PBS), recruiting, and building investor confidence.

Event sponsorship

Sports, arts, festivals, stadium names. The most marketing-oriented form (5.2).

Advocacy and issue ads

Taking a position on an issue. Can come from a company, a trade group, or an activist organization.

Cause-related marketing

Linking sales to donations for a nonprofit partner (5.3).

Issue ads can work as pressure, not just image. After talks with Trader Joe's broke down, the Humane Society ran an ad headlined "Why Won't Trader Joe's Give an Inch?" The customer response was large enough that the chain announced it would switch its store-brand eggs to cage-free within three months. During the vaping debate, the Campaign for Tobacco-Free Kids and JUUL ran opposing issue ads at about the same time.

A positive corporate image cannot be created from a few advertisements.
Belch & Belch, Ch. 17

Belch backs this up with three 2022 reputation rankings: Fortune's Most Admired, the Best Corporate Citizens list, and the Harris Poll's Most Reputable. No company appears in the top five of all three. Apple leads one; Owens Corning leads another; Trader Joe's leads the third. Reputation comes from product quality, innovation, citizenship, and financial soundness in different mixes. Corporate advertising can describe a reputation, but it can't build one.

5.2 Event Sponsorship

Andrews defines event sponsorship as tying a brand to a meaningful athletic, entertainment, cultural, or social activity. It's been growing for several reasons: it gets around ad clutter, it reaches people as media habits fragment, it pleases employees and shareholders, it builds brand equity, and it targets precisely. Professional bull riding attracts Ford Trucks, Carhartt, and Wrangler because its fans are mostly men aged 21–49 living west of the Mississippi.

5.3 Cause-Related Marketing

Andrews draws a precise line. Cause-related marketing (CRM) isn't just a company giving to charity. The donation is contingent on the customer doing something that benefits the firm: buying, redeeming, texting, test-driving. Yoplait gave 10 cents to breast cancer research for every lid mailed back. Campbell's Labels for Education traded soup labels for school supplies, more than $100 million worth over 30 years. If the company gives no matter what customers do, it's philanthropy. If giving depends on a purchase, it's marketing, and should be judged as marketing.

Three things decide whether it works. Fit: the cause should connect naturally to the brand and its customers, otherwise it looks self-serving. Specificity: "a portion of proceeds will be donated" makes consumers suspicious; "$1 per pack, up to $100,000" doesn't. Authenticity: causes built into how the company operates (Patagonia) do better than causes bolted onto a campaign.

Go deeper

Both textbooks present cause marketing as mostly upside. The picture has changed since: high-profile backlashes, a measurable retreat from visible Pride and DEI campaigns, and evidence that younger consumers are less unified on this than marketers assume. See Cause Marketing: Upside, Downside, and the Pullback.

Takeaway
  • Corporate advertising promotes the firm, not a product. It's controversial, but useful for positioning and reaching investors.
  • Reputation is earned through operations. No company leads all three major reputation rankings.
  • Sponsorship checklist: image, audience, misidentification, clutter, fit, and an activation budget of 2–3x the fee.
  • Ambush marketing can win the perception battle without paying for the rights.
  • CRM ties donations to customer action. Fit, specificity, and authenticity decide whether it reads as generous or cynical.

Accountability

Measuring PR (and the Metric That Won't Die)

"No standard effectiveness measures" was on the disadvantages list in Section 2, and it's been PR's weak spot for decades. Without measurement, PR can't win budget arguments against channels with dashboards. With bad measurement, it wins them for the wrong reasons.

6.1 The Bad Old Metric: Advertising Value Equivalency

For years the standard way to "value" coverage was the advertising value equivalent (AVE): measure the column inches or airtime a story got, then multiply by what the same space would have cost as an ad. A half-page article worth $40,000 in ad rates becomes "$40,000 of PR value." Some agencies multiplied the result again to account for editorial credibility.

The problem is that AVE measures the cost of space, not the effect of the message. A negative story earns the same AVE as a positive one. A mention in the wrong outlet, to the wrong audience, counts the same as one in the right outlet. It also tells you nothing about what to do next time.

In 2010 the measurement industry's professional body, AMEC, adopted the Barcelona Principles, which explicitly reject AVE and call for measuring outcomes rather than outputs. Version 3.0 (2020) still states plainly that AVEs are not the value of communication, and added impact as a third level alongside outputs and outcomes (PR News; React & Share). A decade after Barcelona, industry surveys still found AVE in wide use. It survives because it produces a dollar figure, and executives like dollar figures.

Outputs
What we produced

Releases sent, clips, mentions, impressions, share of voice. Easy to count, and easy to inflate.

Outcomes → Impact
What changed

Awareness, attitudes, site traffic, inquiries, sales, donations, policy changes. Harder to measure, but the actual point.

Set an objective, take a baseline measurement (awareness, attitude, sales) before the event or a matched control market without it, then measure the same thing afterward. Colgate-Palmolive's cause-program formula is a model of simplicity:

Incremental profit = (sales in 3 weeks after coupon drop − avg. sales prior 6 months) × net profit margin − per-unit program cost
Corporate ads are often owned by top management, and the people who should care most about accountability are the least willing to be held to it.
Paraphrasing Belch & Belch, Ch. 17
Takeaway
  • AVE measures the price of space, not the effect of a message. The industry formally rejected it in 2010, and it's still around.
  • Measure outcomes and impact, not just outputs.
  • Cision's funnel: brand impact → digital impact → bottom line.
  • Sponsorships and cause programs need a baseline and a post-measure, like any other campaign.
07
Outside the Textbooks

Earned Media in the AI Answer

Neither textbook could cover this. A growing share of product research now starts with a question to ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity, or Google's AI Overviews rather than a list of blue links. Those answers often cite their sources. So a new question for PR: when an AI tells someone about your category, whose words is it repeating?

7.1 What the Main Study Says

The most-cited research is Muck Rack's recurring What Is AI Reading? report, which tracks the links AI assistants cite in their answers.

84%

If those numbers hold, PR's old advantage, credibility from someone else saying it, now has a machine audience too. Paid placement mostly doesn't reach that audience.

7.2 Why You Shouldn't Take the 84% at Face Value

Limitations · Read before citing the number
Five reasons to be careful with the Muck Rack figures
  • The source sells the solution. Muck Rack is a PR software company, and the report is produced by Generative Pulse, its paid AI-visibility product. That doesn't make the data wrong, but it is a vendor study, not peer-reviewed research, using prompts the vendor chose.
  • "Earned" is defined very broadly. Muck Rack counts journalism, academic research, government and NGO sites, Wikipedia, social platforms, and third-party corporate content as "earned." Muck Rack's own staff have had to defend the categorization publicly.
  • By PESO standards, much of it isn't Earned. Under the PESO model, Reddit threads, forums, and social posts are Shared media, not Earned. Separate journalism out and you're closer to a quarter of citations.
  • Other studies find different leaders. Profound's analysis of 30 million citations found Wikipedia was ChatGPT's top source (about 48% of its top-ten citations) while Reddit led for Google AI Overviews and Perplexity. Similarweb found Wikipedia and Reddit each made up about 12–13% of U.S. ChatGPT citations in early 2026.
  • It shifts fast and varies by engine. Semrush tracked ChatGPT abruptly cutting its citations of Reddit and Wikipedia in September 2025. A citation also isn't proof the source shaped the answer, since models also draw on training data that isn't cited at all.
What survives the caveats

Across every study, paid placements are a tiny share of what AI cites, and AI favors sources it treats as independent: encyclopedias, news outlets, government and academic sites, and large communities of real users. The exact share varies by engine and month, but the direction matches everything on this page. Third-party presence is what gets repeated, and you can't buy your way into it.

The practical implication goes straight back to Section 2 and the Earning Earned reading: original data, credible experts, and stories worth covering are now how a brand shows up in AI answers as well as in the news.

For the click data behind the shift, a side-by-side of the competing studies, and what PR teams are doing about it, see Earned Media & AI Search.

Takeaway
  • AI assistants overwhelmingly cite unpaid, third-party sources. Paid content is close to invisible.
  • The headline "84% earned" comes from a vendor with a product to sell and a broad definition of earned.
  • By PESO standards, a large share of AI citations are Shared (Reddit, social), not Earned. Journalism is about a quarter.
  • Engines differ and change month to month. Treat any single number as a snapshot.
  • The direction is solid: the gatekeeper logic of PR now applies to machines as well as editors.

What You Should Be Able to Do Now

You should be able to tell PR, MPR, publicity, and corporate advertising apart, and say which ones a brand controls.

You should be able to explain the credibility–control trade-off and use the remove-the-name test to tell a real PR opportunity from something that should be bought as an ad.

You should be able to sequence a launch across PESO the way Dell did: earn first, then pay to extend.

You should be able to assess a crisis response against Tylenol, Perrier, and the 72-hour window, and fight a rumor with a credible third-party voice.

You should be able to evaluate a sponsorship or cause program on fit, clutter, activation budget, and a measurable objective, and explain why AVE isn't one.

And you should be able to read a headline statistic critically, whether it's "Ronaldo cost Coke $4 billion" or "84% of AI citations are earned," by asking who measured it, how they defined it, and what else could explain it.

You can buy attention. You have to earn belief.

Glossary: PR Basics

Advertising value equivalent (AVE)
A discredited PR metric that values coverage at what the same space or time would cost as advertising. Rejected by the Barcelona Principles because it measures cost, not effect.
Advocacy advertising
Corporate advertising that takes a position on a social, business, or environmental issue in a way that supports the sponsor's interests.
Ambush marketing
Marketing by a non-sponsor that creates the impression of official event sponsorship.
Barcelona Principles
Measurement standards adopted by AMEC in 2010 and updated in 2020 (3.0). They call for measuring outcomes and impact rather than outputs, and reject AVE.
Buzz creation
The organized effort to get people talking favorably about a brand and recommending it within their social networks.
Cause-related marketing (CRM)
A partnership with a nonprofit where the company's contribution depends on the customer doing something that benefits the firm, usually a purchase.
Commercial rumor
A widely circulated but unverified claim about a product, brand, company, or store. Either conspiracy (offensive practices) or contamination (repulsive product features).
Content marketing
Producing, curating, and sharing brand content built around customers' needs that delivers visible value. Owned media unless independently picked up.
Corporate advertising
Paid advertising that promotes the organization, its image, or its views rather than a specific product.
Event sponsorship
Tying a brand to a meaningful athletic, entertainment, cultural, or social activity in exchange for a fee.
Exclusive
Offering a single outlet first or sole rights to a story, to raise the odds of coverage.
Executive-statement release
A news release quoting a senior executive on issues, trends, or forecasts. Tends to run in the news section, where it carries more credibility.
General PR
PR concerned with all of an organization's publics: employees, investors, government, community. Contrasted with MPR.
Internal / external publics
Internal publics are already connected to the organization (employees, investors, community, current customers); external publics are not (media, government, the general public).
Issue advertising
A form of advocacy advertising, sometimes sponsored by organizations with no corporate tie, aimed at drawing attention to an issue.
Marketing public relations (MPR)
The part of PR aimed at customers and prospects in support of marketing objectives. Can be proactive or reactive.
Native advertising
Paid brand content designed to blend in with the editorial content around it. Subject to FTC disclosure guidance.
Opinion leader
A person whose views on a particular topic carry weight within their social network; an informer, persuader, and confirmer.
Proactive / reactive MPR
Proactive MPR seeks opportunities to communicate a brand's merits; reactive MPR responds to external pressures, usually negative.
Public relations (PR)
The management function that evaluates public attitudes, aligns the organization's policies with the public interest, and runs a program to earn public understanding and acceptance.
Publicity
News about a person, product, or organization in the media. A subset of PR; short-term, often uncontrolled, and either positive or negative.
Remove-the-name test
Asking whether a story would still interest anyone without the company's name in the headline. If not, it's an ad, not a PR opportunity.
Video news release (VNR)
Company-produced video packaged for stations to air as news, often without disclosing the source.
Word of mouth (WOM)
Informal communication among consumers about products and services.
