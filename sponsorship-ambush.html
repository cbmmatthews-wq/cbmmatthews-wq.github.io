Sponsorship & Ambush Marketing

A sponsorship buys the right to stand next to something people already love. Whether anyone notices you standing there is a separate problem, and it costs extra.

01
Why Sponsor

Paying to Be Associated

This page expands the sponsorship section of PR Basics. Andrews defines event sponsorship as tying a brand to a meaningful athletic, entertainment, cultural, or social activity. The sponsor pays a fee for the right to the association, then has to market the association. Both halves are required, and most failed sponsorships skip the second.

Andrews gives five reasons sponsorship keeps growing: it sidesteps ad clutter, it reaches people as media habits fragment, it earns approval from employees, shareholders, and communities, it can build brand equity through borrowed meaning, and it targets precisely. Professional bull riding draws Ford Trucks, Carhartt, and Wrangler because its fans are mostly men aged 21–49 living west of the Mississippi. Sport takes the largest share: in Europe, for example, sport accounted for 72% of a record €34.45 billion sponsorship market in 2025.

How big is sponsorship, really?

It depends on who's counting, and what counts

Published estimates of global sponsorship range from about $39 billion (GlobalData's deal volume) to $52 billion (Statista's sports sponsorship plus venue advertising) to $168 billion (Mordor Intelligence's total sponsorship market), all for 2025. The gap comes from definitions: rights fees only, or activation spending too? Sports only, or arts and causes? Deals signed, or revenue recognized?

It's a useful reminder for any market-size figure. Before comparing two numbers, check that they're measuring the same thing.

Big numbers need definitions.

The Prilosec example shows the best case: a big brand in a small, uncluttered event whose audience matches the product almost perfectly. The Coke example shows the worst: a quarter-billion dollars to be confused with your rival. Stadium naming sits in between. Belch reports that 99% of fans can recall their stadium's sponsor and 35% say it improves their view of the brand, but naming rights also tie a brand to a building for decades. Enron Field and stadiums named for companies that later went bankrupt are the cautionary tales.

Two options reduce the risk. A custom event, one the brand creates itself, gives total control over timing, audience, and clutter, and can cost less. And a careful sponsorship agreement spells out exclusivity (no competitor in the same category), what the sponsor can and can't do with the property's marks, guaranteed benefits such as signage, renewal options, and escape clauses for when the team moves or the event changes.

03
Ambush

Getting Credit Without Paying

Ambush marketing is creating the impression of official sponsorship without buying it. The classic case comes from Andrews: after one Summer Olympics, 72% of survey respondents named Visa as an official sponsor, and 54% named American Express. Visa had paid $40 million for the rights. Amex had simply advertised heavily during the broadcast.

Amex paid nothing for the rights. It just bought ads during the telecast.

Event owners fight back with rules. The Olympics' best-known is Rule 40. Created by the IOC in 1991, it protected Olympic logos and slogans and kept official sponsors exclusive. For years it also barred athletes from promoting their personal sponsors during the Games. Before the 2016 Rio Games, Brooks Running protested the rule by driving a billboard truck around the U.S. track trials, and athletes pushed hard for change. The rule has since been loosened: personal sponsors can now run generic advertising, and athletes can thank them during the Games, though sponsors must register and agree to terms designed to prevent ambush marketing. Official partners keep exclusive use of the Olympic marks.

Unethical, or just smart?

Andrews leaves the question open on purpose

The case against: ambushers free-ride on an investment someone else made, and that investment funds the event. If ambushing always worked, nobody would pay for official rights, and the events would lose their funding.

The case for: it's legal, it's cheap, and it neutralizes a competitor's attempt to buy an advantage. Nobody owns the idea of "summer sports," and advertising during a broadcast is just buying airtime.

A useful middle line: creating an association through your own creative is fair competition, while implying an official relationship that doesn't exist starts to look like deception.

Association is fair game. Pretending to be official isn't.
04
Measuring

Did It Work?

The most common sponsorship metric is also the weakest: a head count, sometimes divided into the cost to get cost per attendee. It tells you how many people were there, not whether any of them noticed, remembered, or cared. Andrews' standard is the same as for any campaign:

  1. Set the objective first: awareness, image, sales, trade relationships.
  2. Take a baseline before the event, or use a matched market without it as a control.
  3. Measure the same thing afterward and compare.

The misidentification data above is exactly why this matters. Without measurement, Coke would never have known that a third of fans were crediting Pepsi for its $250 million.

Takeaway
  • A sponsorship is two purchases: the rights, and the activation that makes people notice. Budget 2–3x the fee for the second.
  • Check image, audience, misidentification, clutter, fit, and economics before signing.
  • Small, uncluttered, well-matched events (Prilosec at bunco) can beat giant ones (Coke at the NFL).
  • Ambush marketing can win the perception battle without paying. Event owners respond with rules like Olympic Rule 40.
  • Head counts aren't results. Measure against a baseline or a control market.
